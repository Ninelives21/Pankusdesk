3-phase induction motor: introduction and construction

Introduction

  • An electric motor is a device which converts an electrical energy into a mechanical energy. This mechanical energy then can be supplied to various types of loads. The motors can operate on DC as well as 1-ф and 3- ф supply.
  • The motors operating on DC supply are called DC motors, while motors operating on AC supply are called AC motors.
  • The AC motors are classified as 1-ф and 3-ф Induction motors, Synchronous motors and some special purpose motors. Out of all these types, 3-ф Induction motors are widely used for various industrial applications.
  • The important Advantages of 3-ф Induction motors over other types are Self starting property, No need of starting device, High power factor, Good speed regulation and Robust construction.

Construction

Stator and rotor

Stator

  • A steel frame contains a hollow cylindrical laminated silicon-steel core, reducing hysteresis and eddy-current losses.
  • Evenly spaced slots are provided on the inner periphery of the core.
  • Insulated conductors in these slots form a balanced 3-phase star or delta winding.

Rotor

  • The rotor is placed inside the stator and mounted on the shaft.
  • Its laminated core has slots carrying rotor conductors or windings.
  • Types: squirrel-cage and slip-ring rotors.

Operating principle and rotating magnetic field

Rotating magnetic field

  • Induction motor works on the principle of Electromagnetic induction.
  • The working principle of 3- ф Induction motors is based on the production of Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF).
  • When a 3-ф supply is given to the 3- ф set of stator winding, a rotating magnetic field of constant magnitude is produced. The speed of this rotating magnetic field is synchronous speed, Ns = 120f/P rpm.
  • The current carrying windings produce the magnetic field or flux. And due to interaction of three fluxes produced due to 3- ф supply, resultant flux has a constant magnitude (1.5фm) and its axis rotating in space, without physically rotating three windings.
  • This type of field is nothing but Rotating Magnetic Field (RMF).

How the rotor starts rotating

  • Now at this instant rotor is stationary and stator flux RMF is rotating. So its obvious that there exists a relative motion between the RMF and rotor conductors.
  • Now RMF gets cut by the rotor conductors as RMF sweeps over rotor conductors. Whenever conductor cuts the flux, EMF gets induced in it called Rotor Induced EMF (Faradays laws).
  • As rotor forms closed circuit, induced emf circulates current through rotor called rotor current.
  • So rotor conductors are placed in magnetic field and experiences a force to rotate.
  • As all the rotor conductors experiences a force, the overall rotor experiences a torque and starts rotating, So interaction of the two fluxes is very essential for a motoring action.
  • Hence Rotor starts rotating in the same direction as that of Rotating Magnetic Field.

Synchronous speed and applications

Synchronous speed

The speed of the rotating magnetic field (RMF) is the synchronous speed, denoted by \(N_s\).

\[N_s=\dfrac{120f}{P}\ \text{rpm}\]

Here \(f\) is the supply frequency and \(P\) is the number of stator poles.

For a 4-pole, 50 Hz machine (415 V in the example):

\[N_s=\dfrac{120\times50}{4}=1500\ \text{rpm}\]

Applications of induction motors

  • Fans, blowers and machine tools.
  • Compressors, crushers and conveyors.
  • Cranes, elevators and hoists.

Synchronous speed, slip and rotor frequency

Slip

The rotor speed \(N\) is normally slightly less than synchronous speed \(N_s\). The difference is the slip speed.

\[\text{Slip speed}=N_s-N\]
\[s=\dfrac{N_s-N}{N_s}\]
\[\%s=\dfrac{N_s-N}{N_s}\times100\]

At starting, the rotor is stationary (\(N=0\)), so \(s=1\).

Rotor-current frequency

Rotor frequency

At standstill, the rotor has maximum relative motion with respect to the rotating magnetic field and its induced-EMF frequency equals the supply frequency.

While the rotor turns at speed \(N\), the relative speed is \(N_s-N\). Comparing this relative speed with the synchronous speed gives:

\[s=\dfrac{N_s-N}{N_s}=\dfrac{f_r}{f}\]
\[\boxed{f_r=sf}\]

Here \(f_r\) is rotor-current frequency, \(f\) is supply frequency and \(s\) is slip. At standstill \(s=1\), hence \(f_r=f\).

Induction motor numerical problems

Numerical problems

Worked examples

Example 1. A 4-pole, 3-phase induction motor operates at 50 Hz. Its full-load speed is 1410 rpm. Find synchronous speed and full-load slip.

\[N_s=\dfrac{120\times50}{4}=1500\ \text{rpm}\]
\[s=\dfrac{1500-1410}{1500}=0.06\]
\[\%s=0.06\times100=6\%\]

Answer: Synchronous speed = 1500 rpm; full-load slip = 6%.

Example 2. A 4-pole, 3-phase, 50 Hz star-connected motor has 4% full-load slip. Find its full-load rotor speed.

\[N_s=\dfrac{120\times50}{4}=1500\ \text{rpm}\]
\[N=N_s(1-s)=1500(1-0.04)=1440\ \text{rpm}\]

Answer: Full-load rotor speed = 1440 rpm.

Rotor-frequency example

Example 3. A 4-pole, 3-phase, 50 Hz induction motor runs at 1470 rpm. Find the frequency of induced rotor EMF.

\[N_s=\dfrac{120\times50}{4}=1500\ \text{rpm}\]
\[s=\dfrac{1500-1470}{1500}=0.02\]
\[f_r=sf=0.02\times50=1\ \text{Hz}\]

Answer: Rotor frequency = 1 Hz.

More numerical problems

Additional numerical problems

Example 4. A 4-pole, 3-phase motor operates at 50 Hz. Find (i) synchronous speed; (ii) rotor speed at slip 0.04; (iii) rotor-current frequency at slip 0.03; (iv) rotor-current frequency at standstill.

\[N_s=\dfrac{120\times50}{4}=1500\ \text{rpm}\]
\[N=N_s(1-s)=1500(1-0.04)=1440\ \text{rpm}\]
\[f_r=sf=0.03\times50=1.5\ \text{Hz}\]
\[f_r=1\times50=50\ \text{Hz}\]

Answer: (i) 1500 rpm; (ii) 1440 rpm; (iii) 1.5 Hz; (iv) 50 Hz.

Example 5. A 4-pole, 50 Hz, 3-phase induction motor has 4% slip. Find synchronous speed and rotor speed; then find slip and rotor frequency when rotor speed is 600 rpm.

\[N_s=1500\ \text{rpm}\]
\[N=1500(1-0.04)=1440\ \text{rpm}\]
\[s=\dfrac{1500-600}{1500}=0.6\]
\[f_r=0.6\times50=30\ \text{Hz}\]

Answer: Synchronous speed = 1500 rpm; rotor speed at 4% slip = 1440 rpm; at 600 rpm, slip = 0.6 and rotor frequency = 30 Hz.

Torque equation and torque–slip characteristics

Torque expression

The torque developed by a 3-phase induction motor depends on the flux interacting with the rotor, the rotor current and the rotor power factor.

  • Interacting flux \(\Phi\).
  • Rotor current under running conditions \(I_2\).
  • Rotor power factor \(\cos\phi_{2r}\).
\[T\propto\Phi I_2\cos\phi_{2r}\]

The stator-produced flux is expressed as proportional to rotor EMF \(E_2\):

\[\Phi\propto E_2\]

Torque–slip characteristic

As load increases, motor speed falls and slip rises. Plotting torque against slip gives the torque–slip characteristic.

At a constant supply voltage, torque varies with slip approximately as:

\[T\propto\dfrac{sR_2}{R_2^2+(sX_2)^2}\]

The curve is discussed in two regions: low slip and high slip.

Low-slip region

Low-slip region (stable operation)

For small slip, \((sX_2)^2\) is negligible compared with \(R_2^2\). Therefore:

\[T\propto\dfrac{sR_2}{R_2^2}\propto s\]

Torque increases approximately linearly with slip. At synchronous speed, \(s=0\), so the developed torque is zero. The range \(0\) to \(s_m\) as the stable region.

High-slip region

High-slip region (unstable operation)

For comparatively high slip, the approximation treats \(R_2^2\) as negligible compared with \((sX_2)^2\):

\[T\propto\dfrac{sR_2}{(sX_2)^2}\propto\dfrac{1}{s}\]

Torque decreases as slip rises, so an increased load can drive speed down further. The range the region \(s_m\) to \(s=1\) as unstable.

Synchronous generator (alternator): construction

Alternator

A synchronous generator, also called an alternator or AC generator, converts mechanical energy into AC electrical energy.

Stator and rotor arrangements

Synchronous generator arrangement

Three-phase generated voltages

Construction

Alternator construction

An alternator has a 3-phase winding on the stator and a DC field winding on the rotor.

Stator (armature)

The stationary stator is built from sheet-steel laminations with slots around its inner periphery. Three-phase windings sit in the slots and serve as the alternator armature. The notes state that the armature winding is star-connected, with its neutral grounded.

Rotor (field system)

The rotor carries a DC-excited field winding, supplied through two slip rings. The notes identify two rotor constructions:

  1. Salient (projecting) pole type.
  2. Non-salient (cylindrical) pole type.

Alternator: working principle

Operation

Alternator operation

A DC-excited rotor produces alternating north and south poles. As a prime mover rotates the rotor, the field cuts the stationary stator conductors and induces an alternating EMF according to electromagnetic induction.

The induced frequency depends on rotor speed and the number of poles:

\[f=\dfrac{NP}{120}\]

Here \(N\) is the rotor speed in rpm and \(P\) is the number of rotor poles. The induced EMF also depends on rotor flux and the arrangement of conductors.

Principle of operation

  • The working principle of alternator is very simple. It is just like basic principle of DC Generator. It also depends upon Faradays laws of electromagnetic induction which says the current is induced in the conductor inside a magnetic field when there is a relative motion between that conductor and the magnetic field. As armature is connected to commutator in a d.c generator, armature must be rotating member while field as a stationary. But in case of Alternators it is possible to have Rotating field & Stationary armature
  • The generation level of AC voltage may be higher as 11 kV to 33 kV. This gets induced in the armature. For stationary armature large space can be provided to accommodate large number of conductors and the insulation.
  • It is easier to collect large currents at very high voltages from a stationary member than from slip ring and brush assembly.
  • Due to low voltage level of the field side, the insulation required is less and hence field system has very low inertia.
  • If field is rotating, to excite it by an external DC supply two slip rings are enough. One each for positive and negative terminals.

Practical alternator

  • With the very basic principle of an Alternator, let us now have an insight into its Basic Operational Principle of a Practical Alternator.
  • During the discussion of the basic working principle of an Alternator, we have considered that the magnetic field is stationary and conductors (armature) is rotating.
  • But generally in practical construction of Alternator : Armature conductors are stationary and Field magnets rotate between them.
  • The Rotor of an Alternator or a Synchronous generator is mechanically coupled to the shaft or the turbine blades, which is made to rotate at Synchronous speed Ns under some mechanical force results in magnetic flux cutting of the stationary armature conductors housed on the stator.
  • As a direct consequence of this flux cutting, an induced emf and current starts to flow through the armature conductors which first flow in one direction for the first half cycle and then in the other direction for the second half cycle for each winding with a definite time lag of 120o due to the space displaced arrangement of 120o between them as shown in the figure below.
  • This particular phenomenon results in three-phase power flow out of the alternator which is then transmitted to the distribution stations for domestic and industrial uses.

Alternator: voltage regulation and tests

Voltage regulation

Voltage regulation is the change in an alternator’s terminal voltage from no load to full load at constant speed and excitation, expressed as a percentage of rated terminal voltage.

\[\%\,\text{voltage regulation}=\dfrac{E_0-V}{V}\times100\]

Here \(E_0\) is no-load induced EMF and \(V\) is full-load terminal voltage.

Synchronous-impedance (EMF) method

The method represents armature reaction and leakage reactance by the synchronous reactance \(X_s\).

\[Z_s=\sqrt{R_a^2+X_s^2}\]

Synchronous-impedance tests

Tests and procedure

Perform the open-circuit test (OCC) and short-circuit test (SCC), using corresponding values of field current.

  1. From OCC, obtain no-load EMF \(E_0\) at the rated field current.
  2. From SCC, obtain the short-circuit current \(I_{sc}\) at the same field current.
  3. Calculate synchronous impedance \(Z_s\) and synchronous reactance \(X_s\).
  4. Use the phasor relations to determine generated EMF.
\[Z_s=\dfrac{E_0}{I_{sc}}\]
\[X_s=\sqrt{Z_s^2-R_a^2}\]
\[E=\sqrt{(V\cos\phi\pm I R_a)^2+(V\sin\phi\pm I X_s)^2}\]

Synchronous motor: principle

Synchronous motor

Definition

A synchronous motor is an AC motor that runs at constant synchronous speed, irrespective of load.

\[N_s=\dfrac{120f}{P}\]

Operating principle

  • The 3-phase stator supply produces a rotating magnetic field.
  • DC excitation applied to the rotor produces the rotor magnetic field.
  • The rotor field locks with the stator rotating field.
  • The rotor runs at synchronous speed.

Stepper motor: principle and applications

Stepper motor

Principle

A stepper motor converts electrical input pulses into precise mechanical motion. Each input pulse energizes the stator windings in sequence, causing the rotor to align with the energized pole and move through a fixed angular step.

\[\text{Step angle}=\dfrac{360^\circ}{\text{number of steps per revolution}}\]

Applications

  • CNC machines
  • Robotics
  • Printers
  • Disk drives
  • Medical instruments

Fuses

Fuse

A fuse contains a short piece of low-melting-point metal inserted in the circuit. Overload or short-circuit current heats and melts the element, opening the circuit.

Types

  • Rewirable fuse.
  • HRC (high rupturing capacity) fuse.

Rewirable fuses are described in the notes as low-current devices, with replacement taking time after operation.

Circuit breakers: principles and types

Circuit breakers

  • CB is a switching device which can be operated manually and automatically for the controlling and protection of electrical power systems respectively.
  • During short circuit fault or any other type of electrical fault, these equipments as well as the power network suffers from high stress of fault current, which in turn damages the equipment and networks permanently.
  • For saving these equipment and the power networks, the fault current should be cleared form the system as quickly as possible.
  • Again after the fault is cleared, the system must come to its normal working condition as soon as possible for supplying reliable quality power to the receiving ends.
  • A CB essentially consists of fixed and moving contacts, called fixed and moving electrodes. Under normal operating conditions, these contacts remains closed, until and unless the system becomes faulty.
  • The contacts can be opened manually or by remote control whenever desired. When a fault occurs in any part of the system, the trip coils of the breaker gets energised and the moving contacts are pulled apart by some mechanism, thus opening the circuit.

Circuit-breaker types

  • Miniature circuit breaker (MCB)
  • Earth leakage CB (ELCB) or Residual current CB (RCCB)
  • Molded Case Circuit Breaker (MCCB)

Miniature circuit breaker (MCB)

MCB overview

  • MCB’s are electromechanical devices which protect an electrical circuit from over currents.
  • Over currents in an electrical circuit may results from short circuit, overload or faulty design.
  • MCB is a better alternative than fuse, since it does not require replacement once an overload is detected.
  • MCB functions by interrupting the continuity of electrical flow through the circuit once a fault is detected.
  • Generally MCB’s are designed to protect against over current and over temperature faults (over heating)

Working principle

  • There are two contacts- one is fixed and the other is moveable.
  • When current exceeds the predefined limit, a solenoid forces the moveable contact to open (i.e to disconnect from the fixed contact) and the MCB turns off, thereby stopping the current from flowing in the circuit.
  • When overload conditions exits, the temperature of bimetallic strip increases as excess current flows through it. Hence due to heat generated in metallic strip the thermal expansion of metal occurs which just creates deflection using deflection of bimetallic strip the latch get removed which makes contacts separate there by protect the circuit.

MCCBs protect circuits against overload and short-circuit faults. They are described as resettable devices used in applications with higher current demands.

Major elements include the moulded case, operating mechanism, arc-extinguishing parts, contacts and trip components.

Types of releases

  • Electronic release.
  • Microprocessor release.

Trip-release types

  • Electronic release: In this case a circuit is placed on power device and used to protect devices from over current situation. Since operating characteristics does not depends upon temperature.
  • Microprocessor release: In this case, microprocessor is used to monitor over current in circuit if current exceeds it determining value, The circuit breaker isolates circuit from faulty conditions. This type is used and has several advantages like accuracy reliability when compared with other MCCB.

Wire and cable

Wire and cable

A wire is a conductor with insulation. A cable consists of two or more insulated conductors surrounded by a protective covering.

Main parts of a cable

  • Conductor or core, which carries the current.
  • Insulation (dielectric), which reduces leakage current.

Earthing: purpose and methods

Earthing

Earthing (grounding) is the connection of non-current-carrying metal parts of electrical equipment, or a designated system point such as a neutral, to earth.

Why earthing is needed

  • To reduce the risk of electric shock.
  • To stabilize system voltage under unbalanced conditions.
  • To reduce fire risk due to leakage current.
  • To protect electrical equipment.
  • To protect buildings and machines from overvoltage and related faults.

Earthing illustration

Purpose of earthing

  • Safety: Earthing minimizes the risk of electric shocks by diverting fault currents away from people and objects.
  • Equipment Protection: It helps prevent damage to electrical equipment and appliances by providing a low-resistance path for fault currents.
  • Lightning Protection: Proper earthing reduces the risk of lightning strikes by providing a path for lightning currents to safely dissipate into the ground.
  • Electromagnetic Interference (EMI) Reduction: Earthing helps mitigate electromagnetic interference, ensuring the proper functioning of sensitive electronic devices.

Methods of earthing

Pipe and plate earthing

Two methods discussed are pipe earthing and plate earthing.

  • Pipe earthing uses a metal pipe or rod buried in the soil as an electrode. One arrangement uses a 38 mm diameter, 2 m pipe.
  • Plate earthing uses a buried copper or galvanized-iron plate, surrounded by alternate layers of coke and salt in the illustrated arrangement.

Selecting an earthing method

The choice between pipe and plate earthing depends on soil conditions, expected current, available space and cost. Pipe earthing uses a pipe/rod electrode, whereas plate earthing uses a metal plate.

Pipe and plate earthing

Pipe earthing installation

Pipe earthing

  • Pipe earthing is a type of electrical earthing in which an earthing rod made up of copper-coated steel is buried into the ground.
  • The other end of the rod is connected to the electrical equipment or system through a conductor wire.
  • The rod is typically buried into the ground at least 8 feet deep to ensure proper earthing with maximum efficiency.
  • Pipe earthing is also known as rod earthing, as it use a pipe or road as the earth electrode.
  • The pipe earthing is known for its lower cost and good corrosion resistance than other types of earthing systems.
  • Another major advantages of pipe earthing include quick and easy installation, and less maintenance requirement. Pipe earthing is mainly employed where the soil has low resistivity and high moisture content.

Plate earthing

Plate earthing

  • Plate earthing is another commonly used type of electrical earthing system employed in electrical systems to provide protection against electrical accidents. In plate earthing system, a flat metal plate is buried into the ground which acts as an earth electrode in the earthing system.
  • This metal plate is typically made up of galvanized iron (GI) or copper. The metal is usually buried into the ground at least 4 feet deep to ensure maximum earthing efficiency.
  • This metal plate is then connected to the electrical system through a highly conductive wire. The size of the metal is determined according to the soil resistance and current-capacity required.
  • Plate earthing is mostly used in areas where soil resistance is relatively high and moisture content is low, such as in dry or rocky soil areas.
  • After getting a brief overview of pipe earthing and plate earthing, let us now discuss the important differences between them.

Plate earthing: advantages and disadvantages

Advantages

  • Tried and tested earthing method.
  • Economical and readily available.

Disadvantages

  • Salt may be washed away by rainwater.
  • The plate can corrode, shortening service life.
  • High current flow can reduce the conductivity of surrounding coal by producing ash.
  • Water must be added regularly.
  • Alternative maintenance-free earthing designs are increasingly used.

Batteries: classification and cell types

Batteries

Battery

A battery converts chemical energy into electrical energy through electrochemical reactions. The voltage and current depend on the electrode materials, size and electrolyte.

Classification

  • Primary batteries (non-rechargeable).
  • Secondary batteries (rechargeable).

The electrochemical cell-potential relation is:

\[E_{\text{battery}}=E_{\text{cathode}}-E_{\text{anode}}\]

Primary batteries

  • As the name indicates, these Batteries are meant for single usage.
  • Once these batteries are used they cannot be recharged as the devices are not easily reversible and active materiel may not return to their original forms.
  • Other name for these batteries is disposable batteries, some of the examples for disposable batteries are the normal AA, AAA batteries which we used in wall clock, television remotes etc.

Secondary batteries

  • Secondary batteries are also known as Rechargeable batteries. These batteries can be used and charged simultaneously.
  • A secondary battery or storage battery can be recharged because its chemical reaction is reversible.
  • Rechargeable batteries are (re)charged by applying electric current, which reverses the chemical reactions that occur during discharge/use.
  • Some of the examples for rechargeable batteries are the batteries used in mobile phones, MP3 players etc.
  • There are three commonly used secondary batteries in the industry namely,
  • Lead Acid
  • Nickel- iron-alkaline cell
  • Nickel-cadmium-alkaline cell

Lead-acid battery: working and characteristics

Lead-acid cell operation

Lead-acid cell construction

The negative electrode is porous (spongy) lead, and the positive electrode is lead dioxide. Both electrodes are immersed in dilute sulphuric-acid electrolyte. A separator prevents direct contact while allowing ion transport.

Overall reversible reaction

\[\mathrm{PbO_2+Pb+2H_2SO_4\xrightleftharpoons[charge]{discharge}2PbSO_4+2H_2O}\]

Positive plate: lead dioxide (\(\mathrm{PbO_2}\)); negative plate: spongy lead (\(\mathrm{Pb}\)); electrolyte: dilute sulphuric acid (\(\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\)).

Lead-acid cell characteristics

  • Terminal voltage: during discharge the terminal voltage is lower than EMF because of internal resistance.
  • EMF: a fully charged cell has an open-circuit voltage up to about 2.2 V.
  • Capacity: the amount of electricity delivered before terminal voltage falls to 1.8 V; usually quoted in ampere-hours.
  • Efficiency: the source gives approximate ampere-hour efficiency of 90% and watt-hour efficiency of 75%.

Uninterruptible power supply (UPS)

UPS components

UPS block diagram

Main blocks: rectifier, battery, inverter, static switch and load.

UPS arrangements

Standby UPS

A standby uninterruptible power supply (UPS) switches to battery power when the incoming utility supply is outside acceptable limits or fails. An inverter converts DC battery power into AC for the connected load.

UPS operation

Normal supply

  1. AC mains are available.
  2. The rectifier converts AC to DC.
  3. The DC supply feeds the inverter and charges the battery.
  4. The inverter supplies AC to the load.

Power failure

  1. The rectifier loses its mains input.
  2. The battery supplies the inverter.
  3. The inverter converts stored DC into AC.
  4. The static switch provides the described transfer function so that the load continues receiving power.

UPS restoration and applications

Power restoration

  1. AC supply returns.
  2. The rectifier resumes operation.
  3. The battery is recharged.
  4. The load is supplied through the normal power path.

Applications

  • Computers and data centers.
  • Medical equipment.
  • Communication systems.
  • Industrial control systems.
