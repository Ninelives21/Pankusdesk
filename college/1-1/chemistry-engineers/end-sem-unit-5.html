Spectroscopy and UV–Visible absorption

Spectroscopy studies the interaction of matter with electromagnetic radiation. Absorption, emission and scattering measurements can provide information about chemical composition, structure and concentration.

UV–Visible spectroscopy measures absorption of ultraviolet and visible light. The working ranges used here are approximately 200–400 nm for UV and 400–800 nm for visible light.

Dyes often contain conjugated systems and chromophores. Their delocalised electrons can absorb radiation at characteristic wavelengths. Absorption bands help compare dye classes and monitor changes in a sample.

Dye classIllustrative visible absorption ranges
Azo dyesOften around 400–550 nm
Anthraquinone dyesOften around 400–600 nm, sometimes with several bands
Triphenylmethane dyesOften around 500–650 nm
Limits and pending checks+

The band ranges are illustrative, not unique identifiers for every member of a dye class. UV absorption is not restricted to colourless compounds, and visible colour alone does not establish composition.

Beer–Lambert law and dye concentration

For an appropriate sample and measurement range, absorbance is proportional to concentration and optical path length:

\[A=\varepsilon c l\]

Here A is absorbance, ε is molar absorptivity at the measurement wavelength, c is concentration and l is path length. Keep wavelength, path length and measurement conditions consistent when comparing samples.

  • Prepare standard solutions of the known dye at several concentrations.
  • Measure their absorbance at the selected absorption maximum, λmax.
  • Plot absorbance against concentration to obtain a calibration curve.
  • Measure the unknown sample at the same wavelength and use the calibration to estimate concentration, allowing for any dilution.

This is useful for monitoring dye-containing wastewater and comparing treatment stages. Full spectral scans can reveal changes that a single-wavelength measurement would miss.

Limits and pending checks+

Turbidity, other coloured substances, overlapping bands and unsuitable concentration ranges can interfere. A calibration is needed; the packet contains no worked numerical calibration dataset to solve.

Methylene blue and treatment monitoring

Methylene blue is a model cationic dye used in studies of wastewater treatment. Its colour and strong optical absorption make changes in concentration convenient to monitor.

A shoulder around 605–612 nmMay reflect aggregation, depending on concentration, pH and solvent
Decrease of the main visible band with treatmentDecolourisation or removal of the original absorbing species

Recording spectra over time helps follow loss of the original dye band and the appearance or disappearance of intermediate bands. The method is rapid and relatively inexpensive; sample filtration or dilution may be needed.

IR spectroscopy, night vision and security

IR absorption spectroscopy measures characteristic molecular absorption associated with vibrational and rotational energy changes. The broad IR region quoted here is about 0.7–1000 μm.

IR useOperating ideaSecurity example
Active infrared imagingAn IR illuminator lights the scene; a sensitive camera records reflected radiationIR-LED surveillance cameras in darkness
Passive thermal imagingA detector measures infrared radiation emitted by the scene, without an added illuminatorDetecting thermal contrast between a person and the surroundings
Spectroscopic analysisWavelength-dependent absorption or emission supplies chemical informationGas monitoring or identification of a material’s spectral signature

Night-vision imaging is useful where visible illumination is low. Thermal contrast can assist detection of people and equipment; chemical sensing uses wavelength-specific information rather than a conventional brightness image alone.

Source discrepancy
Page 5 calls IR spectroscopy a technique that “sees heat”, then defines molecular absorption spectroscopy. Thermal imaging and molecular absorption spectroscopy are different measurements; an ordinary thermal image is not automatically a molecular absorption spectrum.
Limits and pending checks+

The claims of all-weather visibility, seeing behind obstacles, the percentage of security cameras with IR LEDs, and detailed airport/military deployments are not established by cited evidence in this packet. Detection depends on the optical path, material and conditions.

IR CO sensing in Pollution Under Control testing

Carbon monoxide is produced by incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels. Infrared absorption near its characteristic C≡O stretching band, approximately 4.6–4.7 μm, can be used to measure it in a gas sample.

In an absorption measurement chamber, an IR source sends radiation through the exhaust sample, and detectors measure transmitted radiation at the selected wavelengths. Absorption measurements and calibration are used to obtain a CO concentration.

  • Collect and introduce the exhaust sample into the measurement chamber.
  • Pass suitable infrared radiation through the sample.
  • Measure the transmitted radiation in the CO-sensitive band.
  • Use the calibrated response to estimate CO concentration and report the result.

Raman scattering and Raman shift

Most light scattered from a sample is elastically scattered: its frequency is unchanged. A small fraction is scattered inelastically, exchanging energy with molecular motions. This is Raman scattering.

ScatteringScattered photon relative to incident photon
RayleighSame energy and frequency
StokesLower energy and frequency; longer wavelength
Anti-StokesHigher energy and frequency; shorter wavelength
\[\Delta\widetilde{\nu}=\widetilde{\nu}_0-\widetilde{\nu}_s\]

The subscripts 0 and s denote incident and scattered light. With wavenumbers in cm⁻¹, the Raman shift is in cm⁻¹. Under this convention, Stokes shifts are positive.

Raman bands provide information about molecular vibrations. Uses include chemical characterisation, tracking chemical changes and comparing biological tissues.

Limits and pending checks+

The source figure uses ν-like symbols while labelling the quantities as wavenumbers. Tildes are used here to make the units explicit. The scattering illustration remains pending integration.

Raman spectroscopy in tumour studies

Healthy and malignant tissues can differ in their protein, lipid and nucleic-acid composition. Raman measurements compare the resulting molecular vibrational signatures.

  • Acquire spectra from tissue using a suitable illumination and collection arrangement.
  • Compare spectral features with appropriate reference measurements.
  • Use multivariate analysis, such as principal-component analysis or linear discriminant analysis, where appropriate to distinguish patterns.
  • Scan several locations to map spatial changes in tissue composition and investigate tumour boundaries.

The applications discussed include brain-tissue studies, breast-biopsy analysis, skin-lesion studies and lung-tissue or sputum research. They illustrate the use of biochemical spectral differences in biomedical investigation.

Nanomaterials and characterisation

Nanotechnology concerns the understanding and control of matter at length scales of approximately 1–100 nanometres. One nanometre is 10⁻⁹ metre.

Nanomaterials have structured components with at least one dimension on the nanoscale. Their dimensions and surface structure help determine which characterisation method is appropriate.

Structures at these scales can exhibit properties different from their bulk counterparts. Characterisation measures properties and structure; STM and AFM provide complementary ways to investigate suitable surfaces.

STM: principle, components and working

Scanning tunnelling microscopy studies a surface using a fine conducting tip brought very close to a suitable conducting sample. With an applied bias, electrons tunnel across the small gap and produce a measurable current.

ComponentFunction
Sharp conducting tipTypically tungsten or platinum–iridium; provides a local tunnelling junction
Piezoelectric scannerControls fine movement in x, y and z
Coarse approach and distance controlBrings the tip near enough for fine positioning
Feedback and computerMeasure current, control position and construct the recorded image
Vibration isolationLimits unwanted movement of the very small tip–sample gap

During scanning, the tip moves across the surface and the instrument records its tunnelling-current response. The current is highly sensitive to separation, and the measurement also reflects the surface’s electronic properties.

Applications include investigating local surface features, defects and surface reactions, studying suitable semiconductor or microelectronic samples, and controlled manipulation of atoms or molecules in appropriate experiments.

Limits and pending checks+

STM images are not literal photographs of an electron cloud around each nucleus. Surface preparation and tip condition affect interpretation. The apparatus drawing is pending integration.

AFM: force sensing and instrument components

Atomic force microscopy senses interactions between a sharp probe and a surface. A tip attached to a flexible cantilever scans the sample, and the response is used to map surface features and study local properties.

  • Probe and cantilever: interact mechanically with the sample surface.
  • Laser and position-sensitive photodetector: monitor cantilever bending or motion through the reflected beam.
  • Piezoelectric scanner: moves the sample or probe with fine positional control.
  • Feedback electronics and computer: control scanning and record data.

In the optical-lever arrangement, a laser reflects from the back of the cantilever onto a detector. Cantilever movement changes the detected signal. Calibration relates that signal to motion and force-related quantities.

AFM can be used with conducting and insulating samples, including polymers, ceramics, composites and biological materials. Depending on the configuration, measurements may be made in air, liquid or vacuum.

Applications include surface topography and roughness, adhesion, friction, mechanical-property measurements and studies of biological structures.

Limits and pending checks+

The probe geometry, environment and operating conditions affect the result. Sample or tip damage is possible. The source instrument illustration is pending integration.

Self-healing materials: principles and applications

Self-healing materials can recover some performance after damage through a repair mechanism built into the material. Recovery may be autonomic, triggered by damage itself, or non-autonomic, requiring an external trigger.

  • Activation: damage or an appropriate stimulus starts the healing response.
  • Transport: mobile species reach the damaged region where the mechanism requires them.
  • Repair: physical or chemical processes restore some of the lost integrity or function.

Potential benefits include longer service life, improved durability and reduced maintenance. The achievable repair depends on the material, damage and available healing mechanism.

Applications discussed include scratch-resistant coatings, corrosion-protective coatings, composites for aircraft and other structures, sealants and adhesives, and materials for difficult-to-access pipelines or cables. Some examples are proposals or research directions rather than established universal products.

Source discrepancy
Page 24 first defines self-healing as occurring without any external intervention, then explicitly includes non-autonomic healing requiring external triggering. The broader classification is retained; absence of intervention is not required for every self-healing system.

Intrinsic and extrinsic healing mechanisms

Intrinsic healing uses interactions or reversible changes within the material itself. Depending on the system, heat or another stimulus may be needed to activate the response.

Extrinsic healing uses an agent introduced into the material. In a capsule-based system, a crack breaks capsules, releasing the agent into the damaged zone where a repair reaction can occur.

A vascular system carries healing agent through a network of channels or hollow fibres. Repeated repair depends on whether the agent can be replenished; depletion limits a simple one-use capsule system.

These mechanisms illustrate why “intrinsic versus extrinsic” and “autonomic versus externally triggered” are different ways of classifying a healing system.

Examples of reversible interactions and reactions include hydrogen bonding and reversible Diels–Alder chemistry. A suitable thermal or other stimulus can permit the healing response; the trigger depends on the material system.

Smart materials and Nitinol

Smart materials change one or more properties in response to stimuli such as temperature, stress, light, electric fields or magnetic fields. Examples include shape-memory alloys, piezoelectric materials and photochromic materials.

A shape-memory alloy can recover a programmed shape on heating after deformation under suitable conditions. Reversible transformations between martensite and austenite underlie this behaviour.

Nitinol is a near-equiatomic nickel–titanium alloy. Its name combines nickel, titanium and Naval Ordnance Laboratory. Its important characteristics include shape memory, superelastic behaviour under appropriate conditions, useful corrosion resistance and recoverable deformation.

  • Medical applications: orthodontic wires, guidewires, stents and selected fixation or surgical devices.
  • Engineering: compact actuators, temperature-responsive devices and vibration-control components.
  • Consumer products: flexible spectacle frames and responsive mechanisms.
Limits and pending checks+

The quoted recoverable strain, mechanical properties, transformation temperature and cycling behaviour depend on composition, processing and test conditions. “Biocompatible” is not an unconditional guarantee for every device or patient. Biosensors: recognition, components and features

A biosensor combines a biological recognition element with a transducer and associated signal processing to measure an analyte. Recognition elements can include enzymes, antibodies, nucleic acids or cells.

PartRole
AnalyteThe substance to be measured, such as glucose
Biological recognition elementInteracts selectively with the analyte
TransducerConverts the resulting change into a measurable signal
Signal processing and displayCondition, interpret and report the signal
  • Useful selectivity for the intended analyte.
  • Reliable, repeatable response and a suitable response time.
  • A useful calibrated measurement range.
  • Stable recognition-element immobilisation and adequate service life.
  • Suitable size, ease of use and cost; biocompatibility where contact with biological tissue is intended.

Classification by transduction includes electrochemical, thermal, optical and piezoelectric approaches. Electrochemical examples include amperometric measurements of current, potentiometric measurements of potential and conductometric measurements of conductivity.

Limits and pending checks+

Not every biosensor is implantable or reusable. Features must match the intended application. The source block diagram is pending integration.

Amperometric glucose monitoring

An amperometric biosensor measures an electrical current associated with an electrochemical reaction. A glucose-oxidase electrode provides an example of coupling biological recognition to a measurable signal.

  • Immobilise glucose oxidase (GOx) in the sensing arrangement near the electrode.
  • Glucose reacts in the enzyme-catalysed process. In the oxygen-based example, hydrogen peroxide is produced.
  • The electrode detects the reaction electrochemically; peroxide oxidation can generate a current.
  • Relate the calibrated current to glucose concentration within the sensor’s usable range.
\[\text{Glucose}+\mathrm{O_2}\xrightarrow{\rm GOx}\text{gluconolactone}+\mathrm{H_2O_2}\]

Important design features include effective enzyme immobilisation, useful electron-transfer response, sensitivity, selectivity, stability and a suitable response time. High surface area can provide sites for enzyme attachment.

Applications include glucose meters, selected continuous-monitoring systems, food analysis and fermentation monitoring. Wearable and implantable implementations require their own design and calibration.

Source discrepancy
The additional DOCX prints “Glucose + O₂ → Gluconic acid + H₂O₂”. This omits water from the overall acid-forming reaction. The main packet’s enzyme reaction produces gluconolactone, which can hydrolyse with water to gluconic acid. Counting atoms gives the two-step and combined forms below; these are PankusDesk equation checks, not a lecturer-approved answer key.
\[\text{Gluconolactone}+\mathrm{H_2O}\rightarrow\text{gluconic acid}\]
\[\text{Glucose}+\mathrm{O_2}+\mathrm{H_2O}\rightarrow\text{gluconic acid}+\mathrm{H_2O_2}\]

In formulas, glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆, gluconolactone is C₆H₁₀O₆ and gluconic acid is C₆H₁₂O₇. The combined equation has C₆H₁₄O₉ on each side, while the unqualified acid equation in the DOCX does not balance.

Limits and pending checks+

The source quotes <5 s response, 1–30 mM range and micromolar detection as illustrative performance claims without specifying a device. They are not universal glucose-sensor specifications. This oxygen/peroxide example does not describe every commercial glucose sensor; no specific device design or clinical performance is inferred. The sensor drawing remains pending integration.
