PDF page 2 · Nanomaterials: introduction, classification and synthesis

INTRODUCTION Nanomaterials are defined as a set of substances where at least one dimension is less than approximately 100nanometers. Nanomaterials are interesting because at the small scale, materials have fundamentally different properties than at the bulk due to increased surface area to volume ratios. The behaviour of Nanomaterials may depend more on surface area than particle composition itself.
• Large spherical particles do not cover much surface area
• Nanoparticles Equal mass of small platelet particles provides thorough coverage (1 x 106 times more) Two principal factors cause the properties of Nanomaterials to differ significantly from bulk materials
• Increased relative surface area
• Quantum Effects These factors can change or enhance properties such as reactivity, strength and electrical characteristics.

CLASSIFICTION OF NANOMATERIALS : Nanomaterials can have one, two or three dimensions in the nanoscale. Depending on the number of dimensions in nanoscale nanomaterials can be classified into various classes. Nanomaterials in one dimension: One dimension is at nanoscale and two dimensions are outside the nanoscale. 1-D nanomaterials exhibit plate like shape. Eg. Nanolayers such as thin films and surface coatings, nanoplates and computer chips. Nanomaterials in two dimensions: Two of the dimensions are in the nanoscale and one dimension not confined to nanoscale. This leads to needle like shaped nanomaterials. Eg. Nanorods, nanofibers, nanotubes & nanowires (carbon nanotubes and inorganic nanotubes). Nanomaterials in three dimensions: Materials wherein all the dimensions are measured within the nanoscale. Eg: fullerenes, dendrimers, colloids and quantum dots.

Synthetic Approaches of Nanomaterials: There are two general approaches to the synthesis of nanomaterials and the fabrication of nanostructures Top-down approaches– Breaking down matter into more basic building blocks. Frequently uses chemical or thermal methods. Bottoms-up approaches – Building complex systems by combining simple atomic- level components.

SCANNING TUNNELING MICROSCOPY (STM)
➢ The Scanning Tunnelling Microscope is an electron microscope that develop three- dimensional images of the electron cloud around the nucleus.
➢ The STM allows the inspection of the properties of a conductive solid surface at an atomic size.
➢ It is a very important technique in determining the atomic structures and electronic states of the surface under investigation.

WORKING PRINCIPLE OF STM In STM, the sample is scanned by a very fine metallic tip. The tip is mechanically connected to the scanner. The sharp metal needle is brought close to the surface to be imaged and a bias voltage is applied between the sample and tip. When voltage is generated between a sample and a tip with a very small distance (a few angstroms), due to the quantum tunnelling there is an electron transition from the specimen to tip or from tip to specimen, which corresponds to a tunnel current at the picoamper stage. This tunnelling current, which occurs when scanning the tip along the specimen surface, is measured and is used to obtain surface topography, since this current is a function of the distance between the specimen and the tip.

Fig 1: Schematic diagram for the working of STM There are two modes of operation- Constant height mode: In this approach the probe-sample distance is fixed. A variation in tunneling current forms the image. This approaching allows for faster imaging, but only works for flat samples. Constant current mode: A constant tunneling current is maintained during scanning.

Fig 2: Different modes of operation in STM

PDF page 4 · STM: advantages, disadvantages and applications

ADVANTAGES
1. STMs are helpful because they can give researchers a three dimensional profile of surface, which allows researchers to analysis a multitude of characteristics, including roughness, surface defects and determining things about the molecules size and conformation.
2. They will activate in temperatures as low as zero Kelvin up to a few hundred degrees Celsius.
3. Scanning Tunneling Microscope works faster than Atomic Force Microscope.
4. AFM max sample size is 150x150 µm. On the other hand, STM generates mm size length and width.
5. Resolution of STM is much better than AFM. DISADVANTAGES
1. STM analyses only conductive materials.
2. STM requires very stable and smooth surfaces, excellent vibration control and sharp tips.
3. STMs use highly specialized equipment that is fragile and expensive.
4. STM requires vacuum atmosphere.
5. STM is very specific technique that requires a lot of skill and precision, difficult to use effectively. APPLICATIONS
1. Scanning tunneling microscopy was developed following the need to obtain local information on morphology, structure, etc.
2. STM can be used to “manipulate” objects on the atomic scale with less damage by pushing and pulling methods.
3. Using the STM nano-oxidation process single electron transistors (SET) can be fabricated.
4. Growth of metal over another metal i.e., alloy formation is possible.
5. Scanning tunneling microscopy offers the possibility of visualizing biological molecules in conditions similar to those in vivo with molecular resolution.

ATOMIC FORCE MICROSCOPY (AFM)
• AFM is a type of scanning probe microscope with demonstrated resolution in the order of fractions of a nanometer.
• Determine the roughness of the surface and thickness of a crystal growth layer.
• It can image non-conducting surfaces such as proteins and DNA also.

WORK PRINCIPLE OF AFM AFM provides a 3D profile of the surface on a nanoscale, by measuring forces between a sharp tip (<10 nm) and surface at very short distance (0.2-10 nm tip-sample separation). The tip is supported on a flexible cantilever. The AFM probe interacts with the substrate through a faster scanning motion. The AFM tip “gently” touches the surface up/down and side to side motion now the cantilever bends, this bending is indicative of the tip-sample interaction force. These forces can be monitored through a laser beam reflected off the cantilever. This reflected laser beam is tracked by a position-sensitive photo-detector (PSPD) that picks up the vertical and lateral motion of the probe. The deflection sensitivity of these detectors must be calibrated in terms of how many nanometers of motion correspond to a unit of voltage measured on the detector.

Fig 3: Schematic diagram for the working of AFM There are 3 primary imaging modes in AFM: Contact mode : Contact mode operates in the repulsive regime of the van der Waals curve Tapping mode: Tapping mode is similar to contact mode. In this mode the cantilever is oscillated at its resonant frequency Non-contact mode: Non-contact AFM mode uses attractive forces to interact surface with tip.

ADVANTAGES
1. AFM provides a true three-dimensional surface profile.
2. Samples viewed by AFM do not require any special treatments (such as metal/carbon coatings) that would irreversibly change or damage the sample.
3. While an electron microscope needs an expensive vacuum environment for proper operation, most AFM modes can work perfectly well in ambient air or even a liquid environment.
4. It possible to study biological macromolecules and even living organisms.

DISADVANTAGES
1. The AFM can image a maximum height on the order of micrometers and a maximum scanning area of around 150 by 150 micrometers
2. Traditionally the AFM could not scan images as fast as SEM, requiring several minutes for a typical scan.

APPLICATIONS
1. The AFM is useful for obtaining three-dimensional topographic information of insulating and conducting structures with lateral resolution down to 1.5 nm and vertical resolution down to 0.05 nm.
2. These samples include clusters of atoms and molecules, individual macromolecules, and biological species (cells, DNA, proteins).
3. AFM can operate in gas, ambient, and fluid environments and can measure physical properties including elasticity, adhesion, hardness, friction and chemical functionality.
4. AFM is useful in tooling studies, roughness measurements and corrosion studies of metals.
5. Polymers: determination of morphology and surface properties, kinetic studies, aging phenomena, surface treatment modifications.

PRINCIPE OF SELF-HEALING MATERIALS: This process involves the following steps
1. Triggering after damage: The first response is triggering or actuation, which happens almost immediately after damage is sustained.
2. Transportation phase: The second response is transport of materials to the affected area, which also happens very quickly.
3. Chemical repair process: The third response is the chemical repair process. This process differs depending on the type of healing mechanism that is in place (e.g., polymerization, entanglement, reversible cross-linking). These self-healing materials can be classified in three different ways: capsule based, vascular, and intrinsic. While similar in some ways, these three ways differ in the ways that response is hidden or prevented until actual damage is sustained.

Types of self-healing materials: Intrinsic self -healing materials: It means that the polymer matrix themselves intrinsically have self- healing properties that can heal after damage occur, although it still need external stimulation. So some materials undergo physical or chemical interaction where rearrangements of bonds occur. Extrinsic self - healing materials: In this condition self -healing material is introduced or pre-embedded into the polymer matrix. Self-healing materials can be further classified as autonomic, where repair is triggered by the damage itself and non-autonomic where repair is triggered by an external action such as pressure, radiation, or heat. In extrinsic self -healing materials the healing agent is introduced or pre-embedded into the polymer matrix. Beside encapsulation a self-healing agent could also loaded in pipeline within the matrix. When broken (cracked), the mechanical force would destroy the capsule or pipeline and triggers the release of the healing agent into the damaged zone and self-healing agent would reach the site of cracking and interact chemically with polymer matrix to close and heal the crack. This method allows for the repair of relatively large damaged areas. However, depletion of the healing agent leads to only a singular healing event. Another extrinsic self-healing system consists of a 3- dimensional network of capillaries or hollow fibers incorporated into a polymer matrix allow for multiple healing events.

BIOSENSORS A biosensor is an analytical device containing an immobilized biological material (enzyme, antibody, nucleic acid, hormone, organelle, or whole cell) which can specifically interact with an analyte (e.g. blood, glucose, urea, drug, pesticide) and produce physical, chemical or electrical signals that can be measured.

Features of biosensor:
❖ The device should be tiny & biocompatible.
❖ The device should be cheap, easy to use & capable of repeated use.
❖ It should be highly specific for the analyte.
❖ Assay should be fast, reliable & repeatable.
❖ The response should be linear over a useful range of analyte concentrations.

CHARACTERISTICS OF BIOSENSORS

1. Selectivity: Selectivity is perhaps the most important feature of a biosensor. Selectivity is the ability of a bioreceptor to detect a specific analyte in a sample containing other admixtures and contaminants.
2. Reproducibility: Reproducibility is the ability of the biosensor to generate identical responses for a duplicated experimental set-up. The reproducibility is characterised by the precision and accuracy of the transducer and electronics in a biosensor.
3. Stability: Stability is the degree of susceptibility to ambient disturbances in and around the biosensing system. These disturbances can cause a drift in the output signals of a biosensor under measurement. This can cause an error in the measured concentration and can affect the precision and accuracy of the biosensor.
4. Sensitivity: The minimum amount of analyte that can be detected by a biosensor defines its limit of detection (LOD) or sensitivity. In a number of medical and environmental monitoring applications, a biosensor is required to detect analyte concentration of as low as ng/ml or even fg/ml to confirm the presence of traces of analytes in a sample.
5. Linearity: Linearity of the biosensor can be associated with the resolution of the biosensor and range of analyte concentrations under test. The resolution of the biosensor is defined as the smallest change in the concentration of an analyte that is required to bring a change in the response of the biosensor. Depending on the application, a good resolution is required as most biosensor applications require not only analyte detection but also measurement of concentrations of analyte over a wide working range.

PRINCIPLE OF BIOSENSORS: Biosensors basically involve the quantitative analysis of various substances by converting their biological actions into measurable signals.

Fig 7: Working principle of biosensor

The biological material is immobilized, and a contact is made between the immobilized biological material and the transducer.

The analyte binds to the biological material to form a bound analyte which in turn produces the electronic response that can be measured.

The analyte may sometimes be converted to a product which could be associated with the release of heat, gas (oxygen), electrons or hydrogen ions. The transducer then converts the product linked changes into electrical signals which can be amplified and measured.

WORKING OF BIOSENSORS:
• Analyte diffuses from the solution to the surface of the biosensor.
• It (analyte) reacts specifically & efficiently with the biological component of the biosensor.
• The reaction changes the physicochemical properties of the transducer surface.
• This leads to a change in the optical/ electronic properties of the transducer surface.
• The change in the optical/electronic properties is measured /converted into electrical signal, which is detected.

SHAPE MEMORY ALLOYS (SMA)

Shape Memory Alloys are intelligent materials that "remember" their original shape and autonomously return to it when heated, eliminating the need for external motors or complex mechanisms. They undergo reversible phase transformations between martensite (low temperature, soft) and austenite (high temperature, rigid) crystal structures through temperature changes. This unique property enables compact, energy-efficient actuation without traditional motors, making them ideal for miniaturised devices and space-constrained applications. Examples: NiTiNOL (thermal), NiMnGa, Fe-Pd, Terfenol-D (Magnetic), CuZnSi, CuZnAl, CuZnGa, CuZnSn(actuator) Characteristics of Shape memory alloys: One-way shape memory effect: (a) Adding a reversible deformation for the one-way effect or severe deformationwith an irreversible amount for the two-way. (b) heating the sample, (c) and cooling it again and (d) With the one-way effect, cooling from high temperatures does not cause a macroscopic shape change. The two-way shape memory effect is the effect that the material remembers two different shapes: one at low temperatures, and oneat the high temperature shape.

Fig 8: One way and two-way shape memory effect Principle: Shape memory metal alloy can exist in two different temperature dependent crystal structures (phases) called Martensite (lower temperature) and Austenite (higher temperature or parent phase). Martensite is the relatively soft and easily deformed phase of SMAs which exists at lower temperatures. Upon deformation this phase takes on second form Austenite. Austenite, the stronger phase of SMAs,occurs at higher temperature. Shape of Austenite is cubic.

When an external stress is applied to the alloy when it is fully martensitic, the alloy deforms elastically. If the stress exceeds the martensite yield strength, detwinning occurs and a large non-elastic deformation will resultuntil the structure is fully detwinned.

The martensite is strain recoverable up to this stage. However, further increase in stress causes the detwinned structure to deform until the external stress begins to break the atomic bonds between the martensite layers, resulting in permanent plastic deformation.

For the austenite phase however,it has a higher yield strength compared to martensite. Initially, the alloy will behave elastically until the stressexceeds its yield strength. From that point onwards, plastic deformation will ensue causing unrecoverable stretching upon unloading.

SPECTROSCOPY

Spectroscopy is the branch of science dealing with the study of interaction of electromagnetic radiation with matter like atoms and molecules. The interaction of EMR with matter gives rise to two types of spectra namely atomic spectra and molecular spectra.

Atomic spectrum arises from the transition of electrons from one energy level to another due to changes of energy in the atom.

Molecular spectrum involves transition of electrons between rotational and vibrational energy levels in addition to electronic transition. Therefore molecular spectrum is much more complicated than the atomic spectrum.

Molecular Spectroscopy provides a clear image of how diatomic and polyatomic molecules interact by looking at the Frequency, Wavelength, Wave number, Energy, and molecular process also provides most useful information regarding the shape and size of molecules, the bond angles, bond lengths, strength of bonds and bond dissociation energies.

Hence molecular spectroscopy is of great use in determining the structure and constitution of compounds.

Spectra

Molecular spectra result from either the absorption or the emission of electromagnetic radiation as molecules undergo changes from one quantized energy state to another.

The electrons in a molecule possess kinetic energy due to their motions and potential energy arising from their attraction by the positive nuclei and their mutual repulsion. These two energy factors, along with the potential energy due to the mutual electrostatic repulsion of the positive nuclei, constitute the electronic energy of a molecule.

Molecules are not rigid structures, and the motion of the nuclei within the molecular framework gives rise to vibrational energy levels.

In the gas phase, where they are widely separated relative to their size, molecules can undergo free rotation and as a result possess quantized amounts of rotational energy.

In theory, the translational energy of molecules through space is also quantized, but in practice the quantum effects are so small that they are not observable, and the motion appears continuous.

The interaction of electromagnetic radiation with these molecular energy levels constitutes the basis for UV-VIS, IR and RAMAN spectroscopy.

UV-VISIBLE SPECTROSCOPY

The UV-Visible range in the electromagnetic spectrum is from 100 – 800nm. The range 100 – 400 nm is the UV range and from 400 – 800 nm is the Visible range.

When molecules absorb light energy, electrons undergo transitions to higher energy states. These absorption patterns reveal crucial information about chemical identity and concentration.

Fig 10: ES of UV-VIS SPECTROSCOPY

In both UV and Visible Spectroscopy, molecules move to an excited state from the ground state only if the valence electrons absorb the radiation. It is based on the principle of Beer Lamberts Law. “When a beam of monochromatic radiation passes through a transparent absorbing medium, the rate of decrease of intensity of radiation with the thickness of the absorbing medium is proportional to the intensity of the incident radiation and concentration of the medium”

A = εcl Where, A = absorbance, ε = molar absorptivity, c = concentration, l = path length

Hence, due to the absorption of light, 4 types of transitions are possible. They are:
1. σ → σ * transition: The energy required for this transition is high (i.e. very short wavelengths; 150nm). The saturated alkanes will undergo this type of transition.

MODES OF VIBRATIONS:
• Molecular vibrations are categorized based on the type of movement of atoms within a molecule.
• Primarily, molecular vibrations are classified into two main types.

1. STRETCHING VIBRATIONS
• Stretching vibrations involve a change in bond length due to elongation or compression of a chemical bond.
• These vibrations are further classified into two types: a) Symmetric Stretching Vibrations
• Two or more bonds attached to a central atom stretch in-phase, moving towards or away from the central atom simultaneously.
• Example: In H₂O, both O–H bonds lengthen or shorten together. b) Asymmetric Stretching Vibrations
• Two or more bonds attached to a central atom stretch out-of-phase, where one bond lengthens while another shortens.
• Example: In CO₂, one C=O bond lengthens while the other shortens.

Fig 15: Different modes of vibration

Stretching vibrations (symmetric and asymmetric) typically appear at higher wavenumbers in IR spectra, usually in the range of 4000–1500 cm⁻

2. BENDING VIBRATIONS
• Bending vibrations involve a change in bond angles or the relative positions of atoms without significant change in bond length.
• Bending vibrations are further classified into the following subtypes: a) Scissoring
• Two atoms or groups move towards or away from each other in the same plane, similar to the blades of scissors.
• Example: In H₂O, the H–O–H bond angle decreases or increases.

b) Rocking
• The molecular group moves in-plane, tilting to one side while maintaining the bond angle.
• Example: In CH₂, the two hydrogen atoms tilt together to one side. c) Wagging
• The group moves out-of-plane in a back-and-forth motion towards or away from the molecular plane.
• Example: In CH₂, the hydrogen atoms move up and down relative to the plane. d) Twisting
• The group rotates or twists out-of-plane around a bond axis.
• Example: In ethane, a CH₃ group twists relative to the rest of the molecule.

RAMAN SPECTROSCOPY
• Raman spectroscopy is a powerful analytical technique used to study a system’s rotational, vibrational, and other low-frequency modes.
• It was discovered by Sir C. V. Raman in 1928 and is based on the inelastic scattering of light, known as the Raman effect.
• When monochromatic light (usually from a laser) interacts with a sample, most of the light is elastically scattered.
• A very small fraction of light undergoes inelastic scattering, where the energy of the scattered light changes due to molecular rotations or vibrations.
• This energy shift provides a distinct molecular fingerprint of the sample, useful for structural identification. Raman Effect
• Raman scattering refers to the scattering of photons by molecules that are in excited energy states, and this phenomenon is called the Raman effect.

➢ In Stokes scattering, the scattered photon loses energy.
➢ In anti-Stokes scattering, the scattered photon gains energy.
• Inelastic scattering occurs due to energy exchange between the incident light and molecular vibrations or rotations.
