Section A · Short answers (2 marks)

A1 · 2 marksClassify fuels based on their physical state with examples.+

Solid fuels: wood, coal and coke. Liquid fuels: petrol, diesel and kerosene. Gaseous fuels: natural gas, LPG, CNG and hydrogen.

Source: Teacher p. 2

A2 · 2 marksList out the major characteristics of good fuel.+

A good fuel has high calorific value, moderate ignition temperature, low moisture and ash, minimal smoke/toxic emissions, low cost, easy availability, safe storage and convenient transport.

Source: Teacher p. 2

A3 · 2 marksGaseous fuels are recommended in the place of solid and liquid fuels. Comment?+

Gaseous fuels mix readily with air, ignite and burn more uniformly, allow accurate flame control and generally leave no ash. They tend to give cleaner combustion, although leakage and pressurised storage require precautions.

Source: Teacher pp. 2–3

A4 · 2 marksWhat is the purpose of refining crude oil?+

Refining separates crude oil into useful fractions by boiling range and treats/converts them into usable products such as LPG, petrol, kerosene, diesel and lubricants. It also removes unwanted impurities.

Source: Teacher pp. 3–4

A5 · 2 marksList out the main steps involved in petroleum refining.+

Desalting/pretreatment; heating and atmospheric fractional distillation; vacuum distillation of heavy residue; conversion such as cracking/reforming; purification (for example desulfurisation); and blending.

Source: Teacher pp. 3–4; process steps clarified

A6 · 2 marksIn the catalytic cracking process, catalyst requires regeneration. Give reason.+

Carbonaceous coke deposits on the catalyst during cracking, blocking active sites and lowering efficiency. Controlled burning of the deposited coke in a regenerator restores catalyst activity.

Source: Teacher pp. 5–6

A7 · 2 marksDefine cracking petroleum. Mention the types of cracking.+

Cracking is the breakdown of heavy, high-molecular-mass hydrocarbons into smaller, more useful hydrocarbons. Main types: thermal cracking and catalytic cracking (including moving-bed/fluid catalytic methods).

Source: Teacher p. 5

For paraffin formation: nCO + (2n + 1)H₂ → CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + nH₂O. Carbon monoxide and hydrogen (synthesis gas) react over a suitable catalyst.

Source: Teacher p. 7

It converts synthesis gas derived from coal, natural gas or biomass into liquid hydrocarbons such as synthetic diesel and waxes, providing an alternative route to petroleum-derived fuels.

Common Fischer–Tropsch catalysts are cobalt (Co) and iron (Fe), usually dispersed on suitable supports. The teacher’s flow description emphasises cobalt catalysts.

LPG is obtained during crude-oil refining and natural-gas processing; CNG is compressed natural gas obtained from natural-gas fields and gas-processing networks.

Hythane is a blend of hydrogen and methane-rich natural gas. Hydrogen can improve flame propagation and partly displace carbon-containing fuel, potentially reducing CO₂ per unit of fuel energy and some incomplete-combustion emissions.

Alkaline water electrolysis (AWE), proton exchange membrane electrolysis (PEMWE) and solid oxide electrolysis (SOEC).

A16 · 2 marksWhy is hydrogen produced by water electrolysis called “green”?+

It is termed green only when the electricity driving water electrolysis comes from renewable sources, such as solar or wind. The electrolyser itself splits water into H₂ and O₂; the electricity source determines the emissions label.

A17 · 2 marksDifferentiate primary and secondary batteries with examples.+

Primary batteries are normally non-rechargeable because their cell reactions are not conveniently reversible (e.g., zinc-carbon dry cell). Secondary batteries are rechargeable via externally driven reverse reactions (e.g., lithium-ion and lead-acid).

Source: Teacher pp. 8–9

A18 · 2 marksGive advantages of sodium-ion batteries over lithium-ion batteries.+

Sodium is more abundant and widely distributed, which can lower raw-material costs and supply risk. Some designs use aluminium rather than copper current collectors; specific performance still depends on chemistry.

Sodium is inexpensive, abundant and available in many regions, and Na⁺ can shuttle between electrodes in a rechargeable rocking-chair cell. Its larger ion size generally gives a lower energy density than leading Li-ion cells.

Anode: Zn + 2OH⁻ → ZnO + H₂O + 2e⁻. Cathode: ½O₂ + H₂O + 2e⁻ → 2OH⁻. Overall: Zn + ½O₂ → ZnO.

A21 · 2 marksLithium-ion battery is called the king of batteries. Justify?+

Li-ion batteries offer high energy density, relatively high cell voltage, low self-discharge and long cycle life; their rechargeable design is widely used in mobiles, laptops and electric vehicles. Cost and thermal safety remain limitations.

Source: Teacher pp. 11–12

A22 · 2 marksWhy is PEMFC preferred in portable and automotive applications?+

A proton exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) operates at relatively low temperature, starts quickly, has high power density and can respond rapidly to load changes; it emits water at the point of use when supplied pure hydrogen.

Source: Teacher pp. 16–17

A23 · 2 marksHow does a fuel cell differ from battery?+

A battery stores a finite amount of chemical energy inside the cell and must be recharged or replaced. A fuel cell generates electricity continuously while externally supplied fuel and oxidant flow through it.

Source: Teacher pp. 15–16

A24 · 2 marksWhat is the function of the proton exchange membrane in a PEMFC?+

The membrane allows H⁺ ions to travel from anode to cathode while blocking electrons and largely separating the H₂ and O₂ gas streams. Electrons therefore pass through the external circuit to do work.

Section B · Essay answers

B1 · EssayExplain the process of fractional distillation of crude oil with a neat, labelled diagram.+

Crude petroleum contains hydrocarbons of different molecular sizes and boiling ranges. It is first desalted and heated in a furnace; much of the feed vaporises before entering the lower part of a tall fractionating column.

The column is hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top. Vapours rise, cool and condense on trays at levels that match their boiling ranges. The lightest gases leave near the top, followed by petrol/naphtha, kerosene and diesel at progressively lower levels. Heavy gas oil and residue collect lower down.

Fractional distillation is a physical separation, not a chemical reaction. Further refining, cracking and purification improve product quality.

Source: Teacher pp. 3–4

B2 · EssayWhat is catalytic cracking? Explain fluidized – bed (moving bed) catalytic cracking method with a neat, labelled diagram.+

Catalytic cracking converts high-boiling petroleum fractions into smaller hydrocarbons, especially gasoline-range molecules and lighter alkenes, using a solid acidic catalyst at elevated temperature.

The preheated heavy oil contacts hot catalyst in a cracking reactor. Hydrocarbon chains break into more volatile compounds. Product vapours are separated from catalyst and sent to a fractionation section. Coke builds up on the catalyst, so spent catalyst is moved to a regenerator where controlled air combustion burns off coke; hot active catalyst returns to the reactor.

The original teacher illustration depicts catalyst circulation. Strictly, “moving-bed” and “fluidized-bed” name distinct reactor configurations; the question combines the terms. State the common reactor–separator–regenerator working clearly in the exam.

Source: Teacher pp. 5–6; terminology clarified

B3 · EssayExplain the Fischer–Tropsch process for the synthesis of liquid hydrocarbons from synthesis gas, with suitable chemical reactions and a neat, labelled flow diagram.+

Synthesis gas (syngas), a mixture of CO and H₂, is prepared from a carbon-based feedstock and cleaned to remove catalyst poisons. The gas composition is adjusted before entering a catalytic Fischer–Tropsch reactor.

Over an iron- or cobalt-based catalyst, CO is hydrogenated to chains of hydrocarbons and water. For paraffins: nCO + (2n + 1)H₂ → CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + nH₂O. For olefins: nCO + 2nH₂ → CₙH₂ₙ + nH₂O. Product vapours are cooled and separated; liquids are upgraded or fractionated, and unreacted gases may be recycled.

The process makes synthetic liquid fuels and waxes from syngas, reducing dependence on crude oil, although the total environmental impact depends on feedstock and energy source.

Source: Teacher p. 7

B4 · EssayCompare LPG, CNG, and Hythane in terms of composition, storage, calorific value, safety, and applications.+

LPG (liquefied petroleum gas) mainly contains propane and butane; it is stored as a liquid under moderate pressure. CNG (compressed natural gas) consists chiefly of methane, commonly around 90–95%, stored as a high-pressure gas. Hythane is hydrogen blended with natural gas, typically around 18–20% hydrogen by volume in the referenced study notes.

LPG provides high heat per unit mass and is common in cooking and heating. CNG has a high methane octane rating and is used in vehicles. Hythane is explored for cleaner combustion in suitably calibrated engines. Hydrogen has high energy per unit mass but low energy per unit volume, so comparing only numerical heating values without specifying a mass or volume basis is misleading.

Safety: LPG is heavier than air and can accumulate near floors; methane and hydrogen tend to rise outdoors. All three are flammable and require leak detection, proper ventilation and compatible cylinders/valves. Hydrogen blends additionally require attention to materials and flame behaviour.

Source: Panku’s Desk supplement: LPG/CNG/Hythane

B5 · EssayExplain the characteristic features of sodium-ion batteries and compare them with lithium-ion batteries in terms of cost, availability, and performance.+

A sodium-ion battery is a rechargeable cell in which Na⁺ ions shuttle between insertion-type electrodes through an electrolyte. During charging, sodium is removed from the positive electrode and inserted in the negative electrode; during discharge, this motion reverses while electrons power the external circuit.

Sodium resources are abundant and geographically widespread, offering a potential cost and supply advantage. Some sodium-ion designs can use aluminium current collectors on both sides. Compared with established lithium-ion cells, sodium-ion cells generally have lower gravimetric energy density because sodium is heavier and its insertion chemistry differs.

Sodium-ion is attractive for stationary storage and cost-sensitive transport; lithium-ion currently excels where maximum energy per kilogram and mature manufacturing matter. Cycle life, cold-weather performance and safety vary substantially with cell design.

Source: Teacher pp. 13–15

B6 · EssayExplain the construction working principle, & applications of a Proton Exchange Membrane fuel cells with a neat, labelled diagram.+

A PEM fuel cell contains a hydrogen-fed porous anode, an oxygen/air-fed porous cathode, platinum-based electrocatalyst layers, a proton-conducting polymer membrane, gas diffusion layers and bipolar/current-collector plates.

At the anode, H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. Protons cross the membrane; electrons travel through the external circuit and deliver electrical power. At the cathode, ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O. Overall: H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O + electricity + heat.

PEMFCs provide efficient, low-temperature, quick-start power for fuel-cell vehicles, backup and portable systems. Constraints include clean hydrogen supply, catalyst cost, membrane hydration and water/thermal management.

Source: Teacher pp. 15–17

B7 · EssayClassify the different types of hydrogen based on their production mechanism.+

Hydrogen “colours” refer to how hydrogen is produced and associated energy/carbon handling, not to different types of H₂ molecules.

Grey hydrogen: natural-gas steam reforming without CO₂ capture. Blue hydrogen: fossil-fuel conversion with carbon capture and storage. Brown/black hydrogen: lignite/coal gasification. Green hydrogen: water electrolysis powered by renewable electricity. Pink (sometimes purple/red) hydrogen: nuclear-energy-driven electrolysis. Turquoise hydrogen: methane pyrolysis yielding hydrogen and solid carbon.

Green hydrogen is often highlighted for decarbonisation, but real emissions depend on electricity sourcing and the full production chain. Colour terminology is informal and may vary by source.

Source: Teacher syllabus; Panku’s Desk supplement: hydrogen classification

B8 · EssayDiscuss the mechanism of electrolysis of water involved in the production of green hydrogen with necessary equations.+

Green hydrogen production passes renewable electrical energy into an electrolyser, splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen. Overall equation for all three technologies: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂.

Alkaline electrolysis uses an aqueous alkaline electrolyte: cathode, 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻; anode, 2OH⁻ → ½O₂ + H₂O + 2e⁻. OH⁻ is transported through the separator toward the anode.

PEM electrolysis uses a proton-conducting membrane: anode, H₂O → ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻; cathode, 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂. H⁺ moves through the polymer membrane.

Solid-oxide electrolysis (high temperature) transports O²⁻ ions: steam cathode, H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + O²⁻; oxygen anode, O²⁻ → ½O₂ + 2e⁻. High-temperature heat reduces part of the electrical energy needed. Renewable electricity supply is what makes the hydrogen “green”.

Source: Panku’s Desk supplement: green hydrogen electrolysis

B9 · EssayWhat is a secondary battery? Explain the working principle of secondary battery with necessary equations. (use Li-ion)+

A secondary battery is a rechargeable electrochemical cell whose reactions can be substantially reversed by applying external electrical energy. In a typical Li-ion cell, the negative electrode is graphite, the positive electrode is a lithium transition-metal oxide (illustrated here by LiCoO₂), and an ion-conducting electrolyte/separator prevents direct electrical contact.

During discharge, Li⁺ travels from lithiated graphite toward the positive electrode through the electrolyte while electrons pass through the load. Negative electrode: LiₓC₆ → 6C + xLi⁺ + xe⁻. Positive electrode: Li₁₋ₓCoO₂ + xLi⁺ + xe⁻ → LiCoO₂. Overall: LiₓC₆ + Li₁₋ₓCoO₂ → 6C + LiCoO₂.

During charging, an external power source drives these reactions in reverse. Li-ion cells offer high energy density and low self-discharge, but require charge management and thermal protection.

Source: Teacher pp. 11–12; balanced intercalation notation clarified

B10 · EssayDescribe the working of zinc-air battery with relevant equations and summarize its features and applications.+

A zinc-air cell uses a zinc anode, alkaline electrolyte, separator and porous air cathode. Oxygen from ambient air enters through openings and is reduced at the catalyst-containing cathode. Zinc is oxidised at the anode during discharge.

Anode: Zn + 2OH⁻ → ZnO + H₂O + 2e⁻. Cathode: ½O₂ + H₂O + 2e⁻ → 2OH⁻. Overall: Zn + ½O₂ → ZnO.

Because oxygen is drawn from air rather than stored within the battery, zinc-air cells offer high theoretical energy density and use inexpensive zinc. They are employed in hearing aids and small devices; air-electrode drying/flooding and limited high-rate performance are challenges. Common button-type zinc-air cells are primary cells.

Source: Teacher p. 10

B11 · EssayDiscuss the classification of batteries into primary, secondary, reserve and fuel cells.+

Primary batteries are normally single-use galvanic cells whose internal chemicals are consumed during discharge; examples include zinc-carbon and alkaline cells.

Secondary batteries can be recharged by reversing cell reactions with external current. Examples: lead-acid, nickel-metal hydride and lithium-ion cells.

Reserve batteries keep a key reactant or electrolyte isolated until activation, giving long shelf life for specialised emergency or defence systems.

Fuel cells are electrochemical energy converters, not conventional stored-energy batteries: a fuel (often H₂) and oxidant (often O₂) are supplied continuously from outside; electricity is produced as long as reactants flow. PEMFC is a common example.

Source: Teacher pp. 8–9, 15–17

30 Dec 2025 · R25Part B Q1 (4 marks) · Describe green hydrogen and explain the generation of hydrogen by Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) electrolysis method.+

Green hydrogen is generated by splitting water with electricity obtained from renewable sources such as sunlight and wind. The chemical product H₂ is the same as other hydrogen; the green label describes its production route.

Construction: a PEM electrolyser has an anode, cathode, proton-conducting solid polymer membrane, catalysts and a DC power source. Water is normally fed to the anode. The membrane transfers H⁺ while keeping hydrogen and oxygen streams apart.

At anode (oxidation): H₂O → ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. H⁺ moves through the membrane. Electrons travel via the external circuit. At cathode (reduction): 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂. Overall: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂.

PEM electrolysis can respond rapidly to variable renewable power and yields high-purity hydrogen. Green status requires renewable electricity; electrolysis powered by fossil-generated electricity does not automatically qualify.

30 Dec 2025 · R25Part B Q2 (4 marks) · With a neat diagram, explain the construction and working of a Lithium-ion battery.+

4 Jul 2024 · R22 (legacy)Part B Q1 (4 marks) · What is knocking of an IC engine? Mention its ill effects & how can it be prevented?+

Knocking in a spark-ignition (petrol) engine is abnormal autoignition of the unburned end-gas ahead of the normal flame front. The resulting pressure waves produce a metallic pinging sound. Diesel knocking is a distinct phenomenon associated with a long ignition delay and rapid burning of accumulated fuel.

Ill effects: unpleasant noise and vibration, reduced power and efficiency, local overheating, and possible piston or cylinder damage.

Prevention for petrol engines: use higher-octane petrol, correct ignition timing, adequate cooling, suitable compression ratio and clean combustion chambers. For diesel engines: use appropriate cetane quality, injection timing and fuel atomisation.

Section B · Essay alternatives (10 marks)

January 2026 · R25Part B Q5 (10 marks)+
(a) How to construct a proton exchange membrane fuel cell, mention the reactions of such a cell at each electrode, and list out applications [5 marks]

Construction: an H₂-fed porous anode and O₂/air-fed porous cathode are coated with platinum-based catalyst. A hydrated proton-conducting polymer membrane (e.g. Nafion) lies between them. Gas diffusion layers, bipolar flow-field plates and current collectors complete the cell.

Working: At the anode, hydrogen is oxidised: 2H₂ → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻. Protons move through the membrane; electrons run through the load. At the cathode: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O. Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O + electricity + heat. The membrane conducts H⁺ while separating gases and forcing electrons through the circuit.

Applications: hydrogen fuel-cell vehicles, emergency backup power, portable electrical supplies and stationary distributed power. Low-temperature operation and rapid startup are advantages; catalyst cost and clean H₂ supply are limitations.

(b) What is Syngas? How Fischer-Tropsch process is useful in synthesis of fuels, explain. [5 marks]

Syngas (synthesis gas) is primarily CO mixed with H₂, produced from sources such as gasified coal, natural gas or biomass. The mixture must be purified because sulfur poisons Fischer–Tropsch catalysts.

Flow: syngas generation → cleaning and composition adjustment → compression → iron/cobalt catalytic reactor → condensation → product separation/fractionation. In the teacher’s process, Co-based catalyst with promoters converts syngas to hydrocarbons.

Paraffin reaction: nCO + (2n + 1)H₂ → CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + nH₂O. Olefin reaction: nCO + 2nH₂ → CₙH₂ₙ + nH₂O. Resulting liquid fuels and waxes can be upgraded into synthetic petrol/diesel, providing a route independent of crude-petroleum distillation.

Source: Teacher PDF pp. 7 and 15–17.

January 2026 · R25Part B Q6 (10 marks)+
(a) Explain the composition, salient characteristics, and uses of LPG, CNG, and Hythane. [5 marks]

LPG (liquefied petroleum gas): mainly propane and butane; liquefies under moderate pressure, burns cleanly when air supply is correct, and has high energy per mass. It is heavier than air, so leaks may pool near floor level. Uses: cooking, heating, industrial and some vehicle fuel.

CNG (compressed natural gas): methane-rich gas (commonly ~90–95% CH₄); high knock resistance and relatively low particulate/sulfur emissions. It is stored at high pressure and usually disperses upward in open air. Uses: cars, buses, industry and distributed gas supply.

Hythane: hydrogen blended into natural gas, commonly around 20% H₂ with 80% CNG by volume for this course. Hydrogen improves flame propagation and may support cleaner lean combustion, but needs compatible engine settings and storage. Uses: adapted natural-gas vehicles and pilot transport systems. Carbon-containing LPG/CNG/Hythane still create CO₂.

(b) How is green hydrogen generated by using water electrolysis? Explain. [5 marks]

Green H₂ is obtained by splitting water with electricity produced from renewable energy (solar/wind). The overall equation for electrolyser technologies is 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂.

Proximate analysis: principle and method

Proximate analysis measures four coal fractions on a consistent mass basis: moisture, volatile matter, ash and fixed carbon.

Moisture: heat a weighed powdered sample at 105–110°C to constant mass, cool in a desiccator and weigh. %Moisture = (mass lost in drying/original sample mass) × 100. Higher moisture lowers useful heating value and increases transport cost.

Volatile matter: heat dried coal in a covered crucible without air at about 925°C for 7 minutes. %Volatile matter = (additional mass lost/original sample mass) × 100. High volatiles affect ignition, flame, smoke and furnace air requirement.

Ash: burn a known sample in an open crucible at around 700–750°C to constant residue. %Ash = (ash mass/original sample mass) × 100. High ash causes clinkers, slagging, heat loss and disposal problems.

Fixed carbon: calculated by difference: %FC = 100 − (%moisture + %volatile matter + %ash). It predicts sustained solid-fuel combustion and coke yield; fixed carbon is not the same as elemental carbon.

Ultimate analysis: principle and method

Ultimate analysis gives mass percentages of C, H, N, S and O, normally alongside ash and moisture. It is used for heating-value estimation, combustion-air calculations and pollutant assessment.

The resulting current, measured at a controlled potential, is related to glucose concentration through calibration. A3 · 2 marksGive the UV-Visible wavelength range and principle.+

The teacher gives the broad UV interval as 100-400 nm and visible light as 400-800 nm; routine UV-Visible spectrophotometry often operates from about 200 nm upward. Molecules absorb selected wavelengths, promoting valence electrons from lower to higher energy states. Quantitative analysis follows Beer-Lambert law, A = εcl, where absorbance depends on molar absorptivity, concentration and optical path length.

A4 · 2 marksWhat is the Raman effect? State two applications.+

The Raman effect is inelastic scattering of monochromatic light: scattered photons exchange energy with molecular vibrations or rotations, causing a shift in frequency. Applications: identifying the molecular composition of materials and detecting biochemical differences between healthy and tumour tissue.

A5 · 2 marksDefine smart materials with examples.+

Smart materials respond predictably to external stimuli such as temperature, stress or a magnetic field, changing a property or shape. Examples include Nitinol (a thermally activated shape-memory alloy) and other shape-memory alloys; self-healing materials respond to damage by initiating repair.

A6 · 2 marksDefine spectroscopy and state Beer-Lambert law.+

Spectroscopy is the study of interactions between electromagnetic radiation and matter, including absorption, emission and scattering. Beer-Lambert law: A = εcl, in which A is absorbance, ε is molar absorptivity, c is concentration and l is optical path length. Under suitable conditions, absorbance is proportional to concentration at fixed path length.

A7 · 2 marksOutline the principles of STM and AFM.+

STM uses quantum tunnelling between a sharp conducting tip and a conducting or semiconducting sample separated by a few angstroms. A bias voltage produces a tunnelling current that varies strongly with tip-sample separation. AFM measures forces between a sharp probe and the sample by tracking the deflection of a flexible cantilever, often with a reflected laser beam and a position-sensitive photodetector.

Tungsten is a common STM-tip material; platinum-iridium is also widely used. Tungsten can be electrochemically etched into an extremely sharp, electrically conducting tip, giving highly localised tunnelling and good spatial resolution.

A9 · 2 marksState the principle of IR spectroscopy.+

Molecules absorb infrared radiation when its frequency matches a vibrational transition that changes the molecular dipole moment. Different bonds and molecular structures therefore absorb at characteristic wavenumbers, enabling identification and measurement.

Self-healing materials repair damage partly or wholly through built-in mechanisms. B1 · EssayExplain Nitinol as a shape-memory alloy, including properties and applications.+

Definition and composition: Nitinol is a nearly equiatomic nickel-titanium alloy. It is a shape-memory material because a reversible, solid-state phase transformation permits recovery of a trained shape.

Working: At lower temperature, martensite is relatively easy to deform by reorientation or detwinning. Heating through its transformation range converts it to the stronger austenite phase and restores the trained shape. Cooling returns the structure to martensite; a one-way material does not necessarily change its visible shape merely on cooling. Trained two-way alloys can remember both a low- and a high-temperature shape.

Properties: large recoverable deformation (up to roughly 8% for suitable compositions), flexibility, corrosion resistance, useful strength, compact and quiet actuation. Limitations include cost, response time limited by heating/cooling, fatigue under repeated cycling and temperature sensitivity.

Applications: dental orthodontic wires, medical stents and fixation devices, compact robotic actuators, and aircraft variable-geometry components for noise or flow control. Refer to the teacher’s shape-memory-effect and Nitinol illustrations.

B2 · EssayExplain UV-Visible spectroscopy and dye estimation using methylene blue.+

Principle: UV-Visible radiation is absorbed when its photon energy matches an electronic transition. The teacher describes σ→σ*, n→σ*, π→π* and n→π* transitions. Conjugated chromophores are especially important in coloured dyes; auxochromes can change the position or intensity of absorption.

Beer-Lambert law: A = εcl. At fixed wavelength and cuvette path length, absorbance increases approximately linearly with concentration over a suitable working range.

Instrumentation: a UV/visible source (deuterium or hydrogen for UV, tungsten for visible), wavelength-selecting monochromator, sample and reference cuvettes, detector (for example a photomultiplier), amplifier and readout. Quartz is appropriate for UV cuvettes; ordinary glass is limited at shorter UV wavelengths.

Procedure: prepare known methylene-blue standards; scan to find its absorption maximum near 660-665 nm; blank against solvent; measure standard absorbances; plot a calibration curve; measure appropriately diluted effluent; obtain concentration from the curve, allowing for dilution.

Applications: determine residual dye concentration and follow decolourisation during wastewater treatment. Limitations: turbidity and other absorbing dyes can interfere; a decreasing dye peak alone does not establish full mineralisation of the pollutant.

B3 · EssayExplain IR spectroscopy and its importance in night vision and security.+

Principle: molecules absorb characteristic IR frequencies associated with vibrational transitions that change dipole moment. IR spectra help identify functional groups and, in suitable instruments, substances.

Night vision includes reflected near-IR illumination and thermal cameras that detect IR radiation emitted by objects. Thermal contrast helps visualise people, animals and equipment in darkness. These imaging approaches are related to IR detection, although not every night-vision camera performs molecular spectroscopy.

Security applications from the teacher’s notes include thermal surveillance, detecting heated objects, analysing explosives and hazardous materials, identifying gases and certain materials, and screening residues or luggage with suitable IR instruments.

Advantages: low-light operation and non-contact detection. Limits: resolution, atmospheric absorption, thermal background, and the fact that thermal images alone do not uniquely identify chemical composition.

B4 · EssayExplain infrared spectroscopy in Pollution Under Control (PUC) testing.+

Carbon monoxide absorbs infrared radiation strongly near 4.6 µm due to its IR-active molecular vibration. The decrease in light intensity across a known gas path is related to concentration.

A typical non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) exhaust analyser uses an IR source, a gas sample cell, an optical filter/detector selected near the CO absorption band, reference or compensation measurement, and calibrated electronics. As CO concentration rises, the detected transmitted intensity at its absorption band falls.

PUC application: a sample of vehicle exhaust is analysed to estimate CO and other relevant constituents with appropriately configured sensors. The technique is rapid and non-destructive.

Important terminology note: the teacher’s heading says “passive infrared detection” and describes PIR sensing, but standard exhaust CO measurement normally uses active-source NDIR. A basic PIR motion detector does not directly measure exhaust CO concentration.

B5 · EssayExplain Raman spectroscopy for tumour detection and biomedical diagnosis.+

Principle: A monochromatic laser illuminates tissue. Most scattered light is elastic (Rayleigh); a small fraction changes photon energy through molecular vibrations or rotations. Lower-energy scattered photons produce Stokes lines; higher-energy photons produce anti-Stokes lines.

Biochemical basis: cancerous and healthy tissue can differ in protein, lipid and nucleic-acid composition. These differences change characteristic Raman band patterns, creating a molecular fingerprint.

Method: acquire spectra from tissue or biopsy locations; correct background and fluorescence as needed; compare band features and apply validated statistical methods such as PCA/LDA or other classifiers; map suspicious tissue regions or surgical margins.

Applications listed by the teacher include brain, breast, skin, lung and prostate tumour research. Advantages include label-free, chemically specific measurements and potential real-time guidance. Limitations include weak signals, fluorescence interference, costly equipment and the need for rigorous clinical validation.

B6 · EssayExplain the working principle and applications of STM.+

STM is a scanning probe microscope that investigates conducting or semiconducting surfaces at atomic-scale resolution. It is not a conventional electron-beam microscope.

Construction: an atomically sharp conducting tip, piezoelectric scanner, bias supply, sensitive current amplifier, feedback controller, vibration isolation and display/computer.

Working: bring the tip a few angstroms from the specimen and apply a bias. Electrons quantum-tunnel across the small gap, producing a tiny current. The current changes strongly with tip-sample separation and local electronic structure. Scan the tip and record the response to reconstruct the surface.

Constant-height mode keeps the nominal tip height fixed and records changes in tunnelling current; it is fast but best for sufficiently flat samples. Constant-current mode adjusts tip height through feedback to maintain current, recording the motion as image data.

Advantages: very high spatial resolution and access to electronic structure. Limitations: requires a suitable conducting sample, sharp stable tips and strong vibration control. STM can operate under vacuum, air or liquid in suitable setups. Applications include surface morphology, atomic defects, atomic manipulation, thin-film growth and nanostructure studies.

B7 · EssayExplain the working principle and applications of AFM.+

B5 · 3 of 8 papersCompare STM and AFM, including principles, modes, advantages and disadvantages.+

STM principle: quantum tunnelling current flows between an electrically conducting tip and a suitable specimen under applied bias. AFM principle: the force between a sharp tip and the specimen bends a cantilever, and a reflected laser beam and position-sensitive detector measure the motion.

STM modes: constant height and constant current. AFM modes: contact, tapping and non-contact.

STM advantages: excellent surface/atomic detail and electronic-state investigation. Its disadvantages include the requirement for a conductive sample, precise tip control and vibration isolation.

AFM advantages: can image non-conducting samples and biological materials, and can work in air or liquid. Limitations include scan-area and speed constraints and the influence of probe interaction with the surface. The teacher lists instrument-specific comparisons; performance depends on the particular instrument.

B6 · 2 of 8 papersExplain UV-Visible spectroscopy for dye-pollutant analysis using methylene blue.+

UV-Visible spectroscopy studies absorption of ultraviolet and visible radiation by molecules. Electrons absorb energy and undergo transitions such as σ→σ*, n→σ*, π→π* and n→π*. Dye chromophores absorb particular wavelengths.

Beer-Lambert law: A = εcl, where A is absorbance, ε is molar absorptivity, c is dye concentration and l is path length. Thus absorbance at a selected wavelength can indicate concentration under suitable conditions.

Instrumentation described by the teacher: light source, monochromator, sample and reference cells, detector, amplifier and readout.

Example: methylene blue absorbs strongly near 660 nm. The intensity of its absorption peak helps measure the dye in industrial effluent and monitor changes during photocatalytic, ozonation or biological treatment. Limits include interference by coloured/turbid samples and overlapping absorption peaks.

B7 · 2 of 8 papersExplain IR spectroscopy in night vision, security and CO detection.+

Principle: molecules absorb characteristic IR frequencies associated with changes in vibrational energy. IR measurements can identify functional groups and certain gases.

Night-vision/security applications described by the teacher: detection of thermal radiation or reflected near-IR light for surveillance; identification of people and animals in darkness; and suitable spectroscopy-based detection of hazardous materials or gases.

B8 · 1 of 8 papersExplain the working principle and applications of an amperometric glucose biosensor.+

A biosensor couples biological recognition to a transducer. An amperometric biosensor measures an electrical current generated through an enzyme-catalysed oxidation-reduction reaction.

The teacher identifies the glucose biosensor as an example and the Clark oxygen electrode as a basic amperometric sensor. The biological element is immobilised near a transducer; the measured current changes with the relevant chemical reaction and is related to analyte concentration.

Supplement (not explicitly developed in the teacher PDF): in one glucose-oxidase design, glucose + O₂ → gluconolactone + H₂O₂; the sensing electrode can oxidise peroxide as H₂O₂ → O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. The resulting calibrated current estimates glucose concentration.

Applications listed by the teacher include routine monitoring of blood glucose, food freshness/analysis and fermentation monitoring.

B9 · 1 of 8 papersExplain Raman spectroscopy and its application in tumour detection.+

Raman spectroscopy is based on inelastic scattering of monochromatic light. Most light scatters elastically, but a small fraction exchanges energy with molecular vibrations or rotations. Stokes lines have lower scattered-photon energy, whereas anti-Stokes lines have higher energy.

Different proportions or structures of proteins, lipids and nucleic acids in healthy and malignant tissues produce different Raman spectral patterns. Comparing these molecular fingerprints can support tumour detection and spatial mapping of tumour margins.

The teacher discusses research applications involving brain, breast, skin, lung and prostate cancers. Advantages: chemically specific, potentially rapid and label-free measurements. Limitations: weak Raman signal, fluorescence interference, expensive equipment and need for careful analysis.
