Introduction and electrochemical cells

UNIT- 2 ELECTROCHEMISTRY

Electrochemistry is the branch of science which deals with processes involving interconversions of electrical energy & chemical energy. It is the study of generation of electricity from the energy released during spontaneous chemical reactions as well as the application of electrical energy to non-spontaneous chemical transformations. These conversions happen through redox reaction (oxidation-reduction reactions) in devices called electrochemical cells.

Electrochemical Cell & Galvanic Cell

Electrochemical cell:

• An electrochemical cell is a device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy or vice versa through redox reactions.

• It consists of two electrodes (anode and cathode) immersed in electrolytes, connected by an external circuit and a salt bridge.

Galvanic cell (Voltaic cell):

• A galvanic cell is a type of electrochemical cell that produces spontaneous electrical energy from a spontaneous redox reaction.

• Components of a Galvanic Cell:

1. Anode – Site of oxidation (electrons released).

2. Cathode – Site of reduction (electrons gained).

3. Electrolyte solutions – Conduct ions.

4. Salt bridge – Maintains charge balance by allowing ion flow.

Example – Daniell Cell:

• Anode (Zn electrode): Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻

• Cathode (Cu electrode): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

• Cell reaction: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu

• Electrons flow from Zn (anode) → Cu (cathode) through an external wire.

Daniell cell

Working Mechanism :

• Daniel cell consists of a zinc electrode dipped in 1 M ZnSO4 solution and a copper electrode dipped in 1M CuSO4 solution. Each electrode is known as a half cell. The two solutions are interconnected by a salt bridge and the two electrodes are connected by a wire through the voltmeter. An inverted U tube containing concentrated solutions of inert electrolytes such as KCl, KNO3 etc., connects the two solutions. This U tube is called as salt bridge, as it acts like a bridge connecting the two half cells.

Anode: Zn → Zn2++ 2e− ... (i) (oxidation half-reaction)

Cathode: Cu2+ + 2e− → Cu … (ii) (reduction half-reaction)

The first reaction is the oxidation half-reaction and the second reaction is reduction half-reaction. The overall reaction is the result of combining the two half-reactions. As a result, if a redox reaction is allowed to proceed so that the oxidation half-reaction occurs in one beaker and the reduction half-reaction in another, the electrons released by the former will be taken by the latter, and a current will flow. This means that the cell is composed of two half-reactions or two half-cells.

The redox reaction can be represented as:

Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu

Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu

Thus, indirect redox reaction leads to the production of electrical energy.

Zn | ZnSO4 (aq) || CuSO4 (aq) | Cu (or)

Zn | Zn2+ (1M) || Cu2+ (1M) | Cu

Electrolytic cell – It is a device in which a non -spontaneous redox reaction is used to convert electrical energy into chemical energy.

Half-cells, notation and electrode potential

HALF CELL:

Half-cell is a part of a cell. It consists of a single electrode, generally a metal, immersed in a container filled with a suitable electrolyte. In a half-cell either oxidation or reduction can occur. If oxidation occurs at the electrode that is called oxidation half-cell. If reduction occurs at the electrode that is called reduction half-cell.

Representation of electrochemical cell

The following universally accepted conventions are followed in representing an electrochemical cell:

Anode / Electrolyte I (conc) // Electrolyte II (conc) / Cathode

The anode (negative electrode) is written on the left-hand side and cathode (positive electrode) on the right-hand side.

A vertical line or semicolon (;) indicates a contact between two phases. The anode of the cell is represented by writing metal first and then the metal ion present in the electrolytic solution. Both are separated by a vertical line or a semicolon. The molar concentration of the solution is written in brackets after the formula of the ion. The cathode of the cell is represented by writing the cation of the electrolyte first and then metal. Both are separated by a vertical line or semicolon. The salt bridge which separates the two half-cells is indicated by two parallel vertical lines.

Single Electrode potential - It is the potential developed when an electrode is in contact with a solution of its own ions, representing the tendency of that species to gain or lose electrons. It is denoted as ‘E’.

(Or)

It can be defined as an element's tendency to lose or gain electrons when in contact with its own ions, causing it to become positively or negatively charged. Depending on whether oxidation or reduction has occurred, the electrode potential will be referred to as oxidation or reduction potential.

Standard electrode potential - It is the electrode potential measured under standard conditions (25°C, 1 bar gas pressure, and 1M ion concentration) relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), which has a defined potential of 0 volts. It is denoted as E°.

The Nernst equation is a mathematical formula in electrochemistry that calculates the cell potential of an electrochemical cell under non-standard conditions (varying temperature and ion concentrations) by relating it to the standard cell potential, the temperature, the number of electrons transferred in the reaction (n), and the reaction quotient (Q). It essentially shows how the actual voltage of a cell changes as the reaction proceeds and concentrations of reactants and products vary.

The Nernst equation is expressed as:

\[E=E^\circ-\dfrac{RT}{nF}\ln Q=E^\circ-\dfrac{2.303RT}{nF}\log_{10}Q\]
\[E=E^\circ-\dfrac{0.0591}{n}\log_{10}Q\quad(298\,\mathrm K)\]

Where:

• E: is the cell potential under non-standard conditions

• E⁰: is the standard cell potential

• R: is the ideal gas constant (8.314 Jmol-1 K-1)

• T: is the temperature in Kelvin

• n: is the number of moles of electrons transferred in the redox reaction

• F: is the Faraday constant (96500C/mol)

• ln(Q): is the natural logarithm of the reaction quotient

In simple terms

• E⁰: is the cell's voltage when everything is at its "standard" (ideal) concentration and temperature.

• The term (RT/nF) ln(Q) accounts for the deviations from standard conditions, particularly how reactant and product concentrations change as a reaction proceeds.

As a forward spontaneous cell reaction approaches equilibrium at fixed temperature, Q increases towards K and E decreases towards zero.

Significance:

The Nernst equation is used to:

• Determine the cell potential of an electrochemical cell at any temperature and ion concentration.

• Predict the cell voltage in real-world, non-standard situations.

• Examine concentration cells, which are important in corrosion studies.

Reference electrodes

Reference Electrode

The electrode whose potential is arbitrarily fixed or is exactly known at a given constant temperature is known as a reference electrode. Using reference electrodes unknown potential of any other single electrode can be found out e.g. two commonly used reference electrodes are standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) and Calomel electrode.

A cell is constructed using the given electrode and reference electrode. Using a potentiometer and standard cell (like Weston cell) the e.m.f. of the cell can be measured. By knowing the e.m.f. of the cell and potential of the reference electrode, the potential of the electrode in question can be easily determined.

TYPES OF REFERENCE ELECTRODES:

Primary reference electrode – (eg) Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

Secondary reference electrode – (eg) Calomel electrode, Quinhydrone electrode

Construction & working of Primary reference electrode (Standard Hydrogen Electrode – SHE):

SHE is defined as the electrode in which pure and dry hydrogen gas is bubbled at 1 atmospheric pressure and 298 K on a platinized platinum foil through a solution containing H+ ions at unit activity.

Construction:

It consists of a rectangular platinum foil connected to a Pt wire and sealed in a glass tube. A glass envelope surrounds the tube carrying the Pt foil. The jacket is closed at the top and open at the bottom. A side arm is attached to the outer jacket through which hydrogen gas is passed into the cell. This electrode when dipped in a 1M HCl solution and pure and dry hydrogen gas is bubbled through HCI solution from the inlet at a constant pressure of 1 atm. Hydrogen gas is adsorbed on the platinum plate and acts as a hydrogen electrode. The electrode is reversible with respect to hydrogen ions.

SHE working and representation

Verified explanation SHE — reference convention +

SHE has zero standard potential by definition; it is not a small measured positive potential. The sign of a different half-cell is assigned relative to SHE and the reduction-potential convention.

Working Principle:

While working, hydrogen gas from platinum plate changes into hydrogen ions and electrons are set free. These electrons accumulate on the platinum plate.

If the electrode is serving as an anode, then the half-cell reaction is

H2(g) → 2H+(aq) + 2e– (oxidation)

Thus, a part of H2 gas changes into ions and goes into the solution.

Electrons released during hydrogen oxidation pass through the platinum connection to the other half-cell. The standard potential of SHE is assigned exactly 0 V by convention.

If the electrode is serving as a cathode, then the half-cell reaction is

2H+(aq) + 2e– → H2(g) (reduction)

Thus, some of the ions from the solution change into H2 gas.

Representation of electrode:

When acting as an anode, Pt| H2(g) (1 atm.)| H+(aq) (1M)

When acting as cathode, H+(aq) (1M) | H2(g) (1 atm.) | Pt

The electrode potential of SHE is zero.

Verified explanation SHE — two separate limitations +

Impurity poisoning and liquid-junction potentials are different effects, not mutually contradictory claims. Platinum can be poisoned by impurities; a suitable salt bridge reduces junction-potential errors. Being a primary reference does not remove either issue. The earlier combined “conflict” flag was too broad.

Advantages:

• Universal reference electrode: It is used as the primary standard for measuring the electrode potential of all other electrodes.

• Accurate and reproducible: Provides a reliable reference point with a fixed potential of 0.00 V.

• It can act as both anode and cathode: Depending on the half-cell connected, it can undergo oxidation or reduction.

• Simple theoretical basis: Based directly on the hydrogen ion equilibrium, making it fundamental and well understood.

A suitable salt bridge reduces liquid-junction errors; use of SHE alone does not eliminate them.

Limitations:

• It is difficult to maintain exactly 1 atmospheric pressure on hydrogen gas for a longer time.

• Difficult to obtain 100 % pure and dry hydrogen gas, oxidizing species and even traces of impurities in gas can poison the platinum electrode (inactive and irreversible).

• It is difficult to maintain the concentration of HCI solution as 1M because due to the bubbling of hydrogen gas through HCI solution, water is evaporated and hence the concentration of HCI solution may change.

• Since it is made up of glass, it is not so handy.

• Platinum used is rather expensive and it is difficult to prepare ideal platinized platinum.

• Hydrogen is flammable and poses safety hazards during handling.

• Not portable - Cannot be easily moved or used outside the laboratory.

• SHE requires large volume of electrolyte solution.

• The potential of the electrode varies when there is change is atmospheric pressure.

Applications:

• Measurement of electrode potentials

• Determination of cell EMF

• Establishing electrochemical series

• pH determination

• Calibration of other reference electrodes

Calomel electrode

Construction & working of Calomel Electrode (Secondary reference electrode):

To overcome the difficulties of setting up of hydrogen electrode the secondary electrode was developed. The electrode potential of calomel electrode is determined by connecting it to SHE whose potential is arbitrarily taken as zero at all temperature. Since calomel electrode is used to measure the electrode potential of all other electrodes instead of SHE it is called as secondary reference electrode.

The calomel electrode consists of a broad glass tube having sidearm as shown in the figure. The sidearm is used for dipping it any solution used for coupling the calomel electrode. At the bottom of the glass tube, there is pure mercury and a platinum wire is sealed into it at the bottom for electrical connections. The wire runs through a separator glass tube to the top of the tube for electrical contact. Above pure mercury, there is a paste of mercurous chloride (calomel) (Hg2Cl2) in mercury. The rest of the glass vessel and sidearm A is filled with a saturated KCl solution or 0.1N or 1N. Sidearm is plugged with glass wool. The glass tube is closed from the top.

Working Principle:

If the electrode acts as anode, it undergoes oxidation liberating electrons and send Hg22+ ions into the solution. The Hg22+ ions would combine with Cl– ions (furnished by KCl) forming sparingly soluble Hg2Cl2. The result is a fall in the concentration of Cl- ions in the solution. Thus oxidation takes place when it is coupled with other electrode having lower oxidation potential.

2Hg(l) + 2Cl–(sat) → Hg2Cl2(s)+ 2e–

If the electrode acts as cathode, the electrode reaction involves reduction; the Hg22++ ions furnished by sparingly soluble mercurous chloride would be discharged at the electrode. Hence more and more calomel would pass into solution. The result is an increase in the concentration of Cl- ions. Thus reduction takes place when it is coupled with other electrode having greater oxidation potential.

Advantages

• Easy to prepare and maintain: No need for gas bubbling or complex setup like the hydrogen electrode.

• Stable and reproducible potential: Provides consistent potential over time.

• Non-polarizable electrode: Potential remains nearly constant even when small currents pass through.

• Portable and convenient: Can be easily transported and used in various experiments.

Ion-selective electrodes

Ion-Selective Electrode (Combined glass electrode)

An ion-selective electrode responds preferentially to a selected ion. Its potential is related to that ion’s activity, but interfering ions can contribute. Selectivity depends on membrane composition and must be considered in calibration. Types of ion selective electrodes:

1. Glass membrane (i.e H+ electrode)

2. Solid-state electrode(eg. F- electrode uses a europium doped LaF3 crystal)

3. Liquid-based electrode(eg Ca+2 electrode uses a liquid chelator)

4. Compound electrode(eg CO2 gas sensing electrode)

Construction of ISE

1. Ion-selective membrane – The sensing membrane develops an ion-dependent potential. It is selective rather than perfectly exclusive.

Examples:

a. Glass membrane → for H⁺ ions (pH electrode)

b. Crystalline membrane → for fluoride, chloride, or silver ions

c. Polymer membrane → for Ca²⁺ or NO₃⁻ ions

2. Internal reference electrode – Usually a silver–silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) or calomel electrode placed inside the electrode.

3. Internal filling solution –Contains a known concentration of the target ion to maintain constant potential inside.

4. Outer solution (sample) –The unknown solution whose ion concentration is to be measured.

5. External reference electrode –Usually a calomel or Ag/AgCl electrode that completes the circuit.

Working Principle

• The working of an ISE is based on the potential difference that develops across the ion-selective membrane due to the difference in ion activity on both sides.

The membrane responds preferentially to the target ion; other ions can interfere.

• Due to unequal ion concentration across the membrane, a membrane potential (E) is generated.

• The overall potential difference (measured between the internal and external reference electrodes) depends on the concentration of the ion in the test solution. According to the Nernst equation:

\[E=E^0+\dfrac{RT}{zF}\ln a_{\mathrm{ion}}\]

where:

• 𝐸= measured electrode potential

• 𝐸0 =standard potential

• 𝑅= gas constant

• 𝑇= temperature (K)

• 𝐹= Faraday constant

• 𝑧= charge on the ion

• 𝑎𝑖𝑜𝑛 =activity (or concentration) of the ion

Thus, potential varies logarithmically with target-ion activity in the ideal Nernstian range; calibration relates the measured voltage to the sample concentration.

Advantages

• High selectivity: Responds specifically to one type of ion.

• Rapid response: Provides quick readings (within seconds).

• Can measure ions in complex mixtures: Works even in colored or turbid solutions where spectroscopic methods fail.

• Portable and easy to use: Simple operation and easy to handle in the field.

• Non-destructive: Does not consume or alter the sample.

• Wide application range: Useful in biological, environmental, and industrial analyses.

Limitations

Direct response is to ionic species. Neutral gases can be measured indirectly using an appropriate coupled gas-sensing membrane and chemistry.

• Interference: Other ions with similar charge or size may affect accuracy.

• Requires calibration: Must be calibrated frequently using standard solutions.

• Membrane aging: Membranes may degrade or lose selectivity over time.

• Temperature sensitivity: Electrode potential depends on temperature; needs compensation.

Applications

➢ pH measurement: The glass electrode (H⁺ selective) is the most common ISE.

➢ Water quality analysis: Determination of fluoride, nitrate, chloride, calcium, and ammonium ions.

➢ Clinical and biomedical analysis: Measurement of ions like Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻ in blood and urine.

Environmental monitoring:

Testing pollutants, heavy metals, and nutrients in water and soil.

➢ Industrial applications: Monitoring ion concentration in chemical, pharmaceutical, and food industries.

➢ Agriculture: Soil and fertilizer analysis (nitrate, potassium, calcium).

The Nature of the Oxide formed plays an important part in oxidation corrosion process.

Metal + Oxygen → Metal oxide (corrosion product)

When oxidation starts, a thin layer of oxide is formed on the metal surface and the nature of this film decides the further action. If the film is

  • (i) Stable layer: The oxide films on Al, Sn, Pb, Cu, W etc., are stable, tightly adhering and impervious in nature.
  • (ii) Unstable oxide layer: Ag, Au, Pt.
  • (iii) Volatile oxide layer: This causes continuous corrosion. MoO₃ is volatile in nature.
  • (iv) Porous layer: Alkali and alkaline earth metals

Metal oxide film formed is:

  • Stable = corrosion is prevented
  • Unstable = no further reaction
  • Volatile = corrosion will be rapid
  • Porous = corrosion will be continuous

Pilling - Bed Worth Rule

A protective and Non-Porous metal oxide layer has volume equal to or greater than the volume of metal from which it is formed.

2. Corrosion by other gases

  • This is due to chemical action of flowing liquid metal at high temperatures on solid metal or alloy.
  • Such corrosion occur in devices used for nuclear power.
  • The corrosion reaction involves either:
  • (i) dissolution of a solid metal by a liquid metal or
  • (ii) internal penetration of the liquid metal into the solid metal.
  • Both these modes of corrosion cause weakening of the solid metal.

WET OR ELECTROCHEMICAL THEORY OF CORROSION

Wet corrosion or electrochemical theory of corrosion or immersed corrosion takes place by the transfer of electrons from anodic parts of the metal to cathodic parts through a conducting solution. It takes place mostly under wet or moist conditions through the formation of galvanic cells and is more common than dry corrosion.

Conditions for wet corrosion to occur:

a) When two dissimilar metals or alloys in contact with each other and in contact with aqueous solution or moisture.

b) When metal is exposed to varying concentration of oxygen or any electrolyte.

Mechanism of wet corrosion:

Under the above conditions, one part of the metal becomes anode and another part becomes cathode.

At anode: Oxidation or dissolution of metal occurs.

\[\mathrm M\longrightarrow\mathrm M^{n+}+n\mathrm e^-\]

At cathode: Reduction reaction occurs, which depends on nature of the corrosive environment.

(i) Acidic environment: If the corrosive environment is acidic, hydrogen evolution occurs at the cathodic part.

2H+ + 2e- →H2↑

(ii) Neutral environment: If the corrosive environment is neutral or slightly alkaline, hydroxide ions are formed at the cathodic part.

½ O2 + 2e- + H2O → 2OH-

Wet corrosion taking rusting of iron as example:

This involves a) evolution of hydrogen and

b) absorption of oxygen

Acidic environment:

At anode: iron undergoes dissolution to give Fe2+ ions with the liberation of electrons.

Fe → Fe2+ + 2e- (Oxidation)

At cathode: the liberated electrons flow from the anodic part to the cathodic part where H+ ions get reduced to H2.

2H+ + 2e- →H2↑ (Reduction)

\(\text{Overall reaction: }\mathrm{Fe}+2\mathrm H^+\longrightarrow\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}+\mathrm H_2\)

This is also shown in the electrochemical series i.e all the metals above hydrogen in the electrochemical series tend to get dissolved in acidic solutions with simultaneous evolution of hydrogen gas.

Neutral environment:

When iron metal is in contact with neutral medium in the presence of oxygen, At anode: iron undergoes dissolution to give Fe2+ ions with the liberation of electrons.

Fe → Fe2+ + 2e- (Oxidation)

At cathode: the liberated electrons flow from the anodic part to the cathodic part, where the electrons are taken up by dissolved oxygen to form OH- ions.

½ O2 + 2e- + H2O → 2OH- (Reduction)

Thus, the net reaction is: Fe2+ + 2OH- → Fe (OH)2↓

If enough oxygen is available, Fe (OH)2 is easily oxidized to Fe (OH)3 or Fe2O3.H2O (rust).

Waterline and galvanic corrosion

Waterline corrosion:

This corrosion is also known as differential oxygen concentration corrosion which is observed in iron/steel water tanks and ships floating in sea water etc. When water is stored in a steel tank, it is observed that the maximum amount of corrosion takes place along the line just beneath the level of the water meniscus. The area above the waterline (highly oxygenated) acts as cathodic and is not affected by corrosion. If water is relatively free from acidity, little corrosion occurs. This type of corrosion can be prevented by painting the sides of the ships by antifouling paints.

When 2 dissimilar metals are in contact with each other a potential difference is set up resulting in a galvanic current. The metal placed higher in the galvanic series acts as anode and the other metal placed lower in the galvanic series acts as cathode. The anodic metal undergoes corrosion and cathodic metal remains unaffected.

Example : When iron and zinc are in contact with each other, zinc which is placed higher in galvanic series acts as anode and corrodes and iron remains unaffected.

Galvanic corrosion can be minimized by placing an insulating material between the two metals and avoiding a galvanic couple.

FACTORS AFFECTING CORROSION

Nature of metal:

• Position of the metal in galvanic series: When two meals or alloys are in electrical contact, in presence of an electrolyte, the more active metal higher up in the galvanic series suffers corrosion. The rate and severity of corrosion depend upon the difference in their positions, and greater is the difference, the faster is the corrosion of the anodic metal/alloy.

• Purity of metal: Presence of impurities in a metal result in heterogeneity and forms minute local electrochemical cells and results in corrosion. For eg. Zinc metal containing impurities such as Pb or Fe undergoes corrosion.

• Relative areas of anode & cathode: When dissimilar metals are in contact, the corrosion of the anodic part is directly proportional to the ratio of areas of the cathodic part and the anodic part. Corrosion is more rapid & severe, if the anodic area is small. The current density at the small anodic area is much greater, and the demand for electrons by the large cathodic areas can be met by smaller anodic areas only by undergoing rapid corrosion. Eg. Small steel pipe connected in a large copper tank.

• Passivity: Metals which are susceptible to corrosion are made passive by alloying with one or more metals which resist corrosion. Passivity is the phenomenon in which a metal or alloy exhibits a much higher corrosion resistance than expected from its position in the galvanic series. Passivation of metal takes place only in certain environments, which tend to maintain protective film on the surface.

Passivity results in the formation of highly protective, very thin (0.0004mm thick) and quite invisible film on the surface of metal or an alloy, which makes it more noble. This film is insoluble, non-porous and self-healing in nature (repairs itself on re-exposure to oxidizing conditions).

Examples:

1. Aluminum & iron are not attacked by conc HNO3, they become chemically inert or passive due to the formation of a layer of iron oxide (Fe3O4) & Al2O3 on their surface.

2. But iron gets rapidly corroded by diluted HNO3.

3. Iron is rendered passive by alloying it with any transition metals such as chromium, nickel, and molybdenum. Presence of Cr maintains protective oxide film hence exhibits high corrosion resistance in HNO3 solution.

Passive metals and alloys: Titanium, aluminum, chromium, thallium, and a wide variety of stainless-steel containing chromium. These exhibit outstanding corrosion resistance in oxidizing environments, but in reducing environments, they become chemically active.

Environmental factors and control overview

Nature of environment:

• Humidity: Corrosion of metal is faster in humid environments as gases like CO2, O2 etc. present in the atmosphere combine with water vapour and furnish local electrochemical cells.

• Temperature: Increase in temperature results in enhancing the rate of reaction as well as diffusion rate thereby increasing corrosion.

• Influence of pH: Acidic media are more corrosive than alkaline and neutral media. Corrosion of metals readily attacked by acid can be reduced by increasing the pH of the attacking environment. Eg. Zinc rapidly corrodes even in weakly acidic solutions(pH<5) but suffers minimum corrosion at pH=11.

Control of Corrosion:

• Proper designing

• Use of high purity metals

Cathodic protection and sacrificial anodes

CATHODIC PROTECTION

Corrosion control methods

Cathodic protection

The method of protecting the base metal by making it to behave like a cathode is called as cathodic protection. There are two types of cathodic protection

  • (a) Sacrificial anode method
  • (b) Impressed current method.

a. Sacrificial anode method

  • In this protection method, the metallic structure to be protected (base metal) is connected by a wire to a more anodic metal so that all the corrosion is concentrated at this more anodic metal.
  • The more anodic metal itself gets corroded slowly, while the parent structure (cathodic) is protected. The more active metal so employed is called sacrificial anode. The corroded sacrificial anode is replaced by a fresh one, when consumed completely.
  • Metals commonly employed as sacrificial anode are Mg, Zn, Al and their alloys which possess low reduction potential and occupies higher end in electrochemical series.

Eg. A ship-hull which is made up of steel is connected to sacrificial anode (Zn-blocks) which undergoes corrosion leaving the base metal protected.

The underground water pipelines and water tanks are also protected by sacrificial anode method. By referring to the electrochemical series, the metal with low reduction potential is connected to the base metal which acts as anode.

Source correction
A sacrificial system supplies galvanic current without an external DC source. Limited driving voltage can make it unsuitable where current demand or electrolyte resistance is high; consumption requires replacement and monitoring. Thus “uncoated parts cannot be protected” and “high starting current is required” are not general limitations.

Applications:

Suitable for applications / structures that are buried / placed in the soil with low resistivity

  • 1) Protection of buried pipelines
  • 2) underground cables
  • 3) water tanks
  • 4) marine corrosion of cables, ship-hulls and piers
  • 5) Insertion of magnesium sheets into the domestic water boilers to prevent the formation of rust.
  • 6) Calcium metal is employed to minimize engine corrosion.

Advantages:

  • 1) Low installation and operating cost.
  • 2) Capacity to protect complex structures.
  • 3) Applied to wide range of severe corrodents.

Limitations:

  • 1) Limited driving voltage may not supply sufficient current for high-demand structures.
  • 2) Large bare areas require greater protective current and anode capacity.
  • 3) Frequent monitoring and replacement of sacrificial anode block should be done
  • 4) Limited driving potential, hence, not applicable for large objects.

Protection applications and impressed current

b. Impressed current method

For larger structures, galvanic anodes cannot economically deliver enough current to provide complete protection.

  • In this method, an impressed current is applied in opposite direction to nullify the corrosion current, and convert the corroding metal from anode to cathode
  • The impressed current is slightly higher than the corrosion current. Thus the anodic corroding metal becomes cathodic and protected from corrosion.
  • The impressed current is taken from a battery or rectified on A.C. line.
  • The anode is usually insoluble anode like graphite, high silica iron, scrap iron, stainless steel, or platinum.
  • A sufficient DC current is applied to an inert anode, buried in the soil (or immersed in the corroding medium) and connected to the metallic structure to be protected.
  • The anode is, usually, a back fill, composed of coke breeze or gypsum, so as to increase the electrical contact with the surrounding soil.

The impressed current protection method is used for water tanks, water & oil pipelines, transmission line towers etc.

Limitations:

  • Initial costs can be more expensive.
  • Requires an external DC power source along with an AC supply.
  • System requires routine maintenance and monitoring.
  • Anode wires can be susceptible to damage.
  • This kind of protection technique is particularly useful for large structures for long term operations

Advantages:

  • Driving voltage and current can be adjusted within the power supply and system design limits.
  • Capable of protecting large steel structures when designed properly.
  • Requires less anodes then a galvanic system.
  • Output can be controlled using a permanent reference electrode, desirable when the electrolyte resistivity is known to change.

Choosing a reference electrode

Without a proper understanding of reference electrodes, all electrochemical data becomes unreliable, and the bridge between laboratory science and real-world technology collapses.

We study reference electrodes because they are the indispensable, silent partners in any electrochemical measurement. They provide the stable foundation that allows us to:

  • Diagnose medical conditions with a blood test.
  • Prevent environmental disasters by monitoring pipeline corrosion.
  • Develop new batteries and materials in the lab.
  • Monitor our health with wearable technology.

Essay 1 · Construction and working of a galvanic cell Explain the construction and working of an electrochemical (galvanic) cell with a neat, labelled diagram. +
PankusDesk answer

A galvanic cell converts the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy. In the Daniell cell, a zinc electrode is immersed in ZnSO₄ solution and a copper electrode in CuSO₄ solution. A salt bridge containing an inert electrolyte joins the half-cells, and an external wire with a voltmeter/load joins the metal electrodes.

The cell notation is \(\mathrm{Zn}|\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}(1\,\mathrm M)||\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(1\,\mathrm M)|\mathrm{Cu}\). Zinc is the negative anode and copper the positive cathode.

At the anode: \(\mathrm{Zn}\rightarrow\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}+2\mathrm e^-\). At the cathode: \(\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}+2\mathrm e^-\rightarrow\mathrm{Cu}\). Adding the half-reactions gives \(\mathrm{Zn}+\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}\rightarrow\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}+\mathrm{Cu}\).

Electrons pass through the external wire from zinc to copper. The salt bridge carries ionic current and maintains charge balance: anions move towards the zinc half-cell as Zn²⁺ forms, and cations towards the copper half-cell as Cu²⁺ is consumed. Oxidation and reduction remain spatially separated but electrically coupled.

The labelled Daniell-cell diagram below shows the separated half-cells, salt bridge and electron path.

Final answer: Chemical → electrical energy; zinc oxidizes, copper ions reduce, and electrons flow externally from Zn to Cu.

Essay 5 · Wet corrosion and rusting Explain the mechanism involved in the rusting of iron by the wet theory of corrosion./ Discuss in detail the mechanism of electrochemical corrosion theory with the evolution of hydrogen and absorption of oxygen. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Wet corrosion is electrochemical attack in a conducting aqueous or moist environment. Different metals, impurities or unequal oxygen access create anodic and cathodic regions. Metal dissolution occurs at the anode; reduction occurs at the cathode.

For iron the anodic reaction is \(\mathrm{Fe}\rightarrow\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}+2\mathrm e^-\). Electrons pass through the metal to cathodic regions; ionic conduction through the electrolyte completes the cell.

In an acidic environment, the cathodic reaction is hydrogen evolution: \(2\mathrm H^++2\mathrm e^-\rightarrow\mathrm H_2\uparrow\). Adding the half-reactions gives \(\mathrm{Fe}+2\mathrm H^+\rightarrow\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}+\mathrm H_2\uparrow\).

In neutral/slightly alkaline aerated water, oxygen reduction occurs: \(\dfrac12\mathrm O_2+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O+2\mathrm e^-\rightarrow2\mathrm{OH}^-\). Ferrous ions then precipitate: \(\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}+2\mathrm{OH}^-\rightarrow\mathrm{Fe(OH)}_2\).

Further oxidation gives \(4\mathrm{Fe(OH)}_2+\mathrm O_2+2\mathrm H_2\mathrm O\rightarrow4\mathrm{Fe(OH)}_3\). Rust is hydrated ferric oxide/oxyhydroxide material; the supplied notes also write hydrated Fe₂O₃. It does not form a durable protective barrier.

Hydrogen evolution and oxygen absorption are alternative cathodic mechanisms depending on environment. The anodic region loses iron in both cases; the cathodic region supports reduction.

Differential-aeration corrosion arises from unequal oxygen concentration at different parts of the same metal surface. Less-aerated regions become anodic and dissolve; more-aerated regions become cathodic and support oxygen reduction.

In a partially filled steel tank, oxygen reaches the surface/meniscus region more easily than the submerged region just beneath it. The less-aerated submerged area becomes anodic. Under a dust particle, deposit or damaged protective film, oxygen access is restricted. The covered small region becomes an anode while the larger exposed surface is cathodic. Metal dissolves locally and a pit develops.

The notes give machinery parts, oil-tank bottoms and chloride-exposed stainless steel, titanium or aluminium alloys as examples.
