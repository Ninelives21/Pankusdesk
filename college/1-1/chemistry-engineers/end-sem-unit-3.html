Fuels and characteristics of a good fuel

A fuel is a combustible substance that releases useful heat energy on oxidation. Fuels are used for domestic and industrial heating and for conversion of heat into other forms of energy.

  • High calorific value: a large amount of heat should be released per unit quantity.
  • Moderate ignition temperature and a controllable rate of combustion.
  • Low moisture content and little ash or other unwanted residue.
  • Low emission of smoke and harmful gases such as carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide and hydrogen sulphide.
  • Safe, convenient transport and storage, with reasonable handling and storage costs.
Physical stateExamples
SolidWood and coal; charcoal is a manufactured solid fuel.
LiquidCrude petroleum; refined petrol, diesel and kerosene.
GaseousNatural gas, LPG, CNG, coal gas, producer gas and water gas.

Petroleum and refining of crude oil

Petroleum is a mixture of hydrocarbons, including straight-chain paraffins, cycloparaffins (naphthenes), olefins and aromatic compounds, together with smaller amounts of compounds containing oxygen, nitrogen and sulphur.

ElementTypical composition range
Carbon80–87%
Hydrogen11.1–15%
Sulphur0.1–3.5%
Oxygen0.1–0.9%
Nitrogen0.4–0.9%

Refining removes unwanted impurities and separates crude petroleum into useful fractions with different boiling ranges. A simplified refining sequence is:

  • Remove solid impurities by filtration.
  • Remove emulsified water by electrical treatment in Cottrell’s apparatus. Water droplets coalesce into larger drops and separate from the oil.
  • One treatment for sulphur compounds uses copper oxide; the resulting copper sulphide is removed.
  • Heat the crude to about 400°C. Pass the vapours into a fractionating column with trays and bubble caps. As vapours rise and cool, higher-boiling fractions condense lower in the column and lower-boiling fractions condense higher up.

The fractions listed are uncondensed gases, petroleum ether, gasoline, naphtha, kerosene, diesel and heavy oil. Further separation of heavy oil gives lubricating oil, petroleum jelly, grease and paraffin wax; the heavy residue includes asphalt.

Straight-run petrol is gasoline obtained directly by fractional distillation of crude petroleum.

Source discrepancy
The source calls cycloparaffins “naphthalene”. The intended class is naphthenes (cycloalkanes); naphthalene is an aromatic compound, not a name for cycloparaffins.

Cracking and fluidized-bed catalytic cracking

Additional explanation · PankusDeskWhat the additional cracking slides contribute+

The slides emphasise improved contact between feed and catalyst, uniform temperature and efficient heat transfer in the described fluidised-bed process. They also identify catalyst regeneration and reuse as advantages. These points can supplement the existing reactor–cyclone–regenerator explanation.

The supplied slide 11 uses “fluidised-bed” and “moving-bed” together. Preserve that course-source terminology when citing the handout, but do not treat it as a universal equivalence between every process bearing those names. A process diagram must be checked against the mechanism actually being described.

Based on 2 UNIT III Part 2 Cracking (1).pdf · PDF pages 6, 7, 8, 11. Source and remaining checks. Existing answers and figures are unchanged.

Cracking breaks heavier hydrocarbon molecules into smaller hydrocarbons using heat, a catalyst, or both. One balanced example is:

\[\mathrm{C_{10}H_{22}\rightarrow C_5H_{12}+C_5H_{10}}\]

Thermal cracking is classified into liquid-phase and vapour-phase processes. Catalytic cracking includes fixed-bed and fluidized-bed processes. The course terminology also calls this moving-bed catalytic cracking.

In the described fluidized-bed process, preheated heavy oil vapour mixes with finely powdered catalyst such as aluminium silicate or alumina. The powder behaves as a fluid in the gas stream. Cracking occurs in the reactor at about 500°C and 3–5 kg/cm².

  • A cyclone separates catalyst particles from cracked oil vapours. The vapours pass to a fractionating column.
  • Carbon deposits on the catalyst reduce its activity. Spent catalyst passes to a regenerator, described at about 600°C, where deposited carbon is burned off.
  • Regenerated catalyst circulates back to contact fresh feed. The heat released in regeneration helps supply the cracking process.

Advantages include improved gasoline yield and quality, selective and faster reactions, and control over products. The product contains more aromatics and isoparaffins and fewer gum-forming diolefins.

Source discrepancy
One advantage in the source says the heat comes from burning “coal embedded in the catalyst”. The process description on the same page identifies deposited carbon (coke), not separately embedded coal.

Synthetic fuel: Fischer–Tropsch process

The Fischer–Tropsch process converts synthesis gas, a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen, into hydrocarbons over a catalyst. The synthesis gas can be obtained from coal, natural gas or biomass.

The historical route first forms water gas by passing steam over red-hot coke. The gas is purified to remove sulphur compounds before contacting the synthesis catalyst.

In this historical example, purified gas is compressed to 5–25 atm and passed through a converter at 200–300°C. The catalyst formulation is 100 parts cobalt, 5 parts thoria, 8 parts magnesia and 200 parts kieselguhr support.

\[n\mathrm{CO}+2n\mathrm{H_2}\rightarrow\mathrm{C_{n}H_{2n}}+n\mathrm{H_2O}\]
\[n\mathrm{CO}+(2n+1)\mathrm{H_2}\rightarrow\mathrm{C_{n}H_{2n+2}}+n\mathrm{H_2O}\]

The first equation represents olefin formation and the second paraffin formation. The reactions are exothermic. The outgoing mixture is cooled, the liquid product is separated and fractionated, and heavy oil can be sent for cracking.

ComponentRole
CobaltActive catalytic metal
ThoriaPromoter
MagnesiaStructural promoter
KieselguhrHigh-surface-area support

Other formulations use iron-based catalysts and alternative supports and promoters. The listed historical formulation is an example, not the only Fischer–Tropsch catalyst.

Green hydrogen and water electrolysis

Green hydrogen is hydrogen produced by water electrolysis using electricity from renewable sources. Water electrolysis itself does not establish that the hydrogen is green: the electricity source matters.

An electrolyser uses direct current to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. The electrodes are separated by a suitable electrolyte and separator or membrane. Hydrogen is collected at the cathode and oxygen at the anode.

\[2\mathrm{H_2O}\rightarrow 2\mathrm{H_2}+\mathrm{O_2}\]
TypeElectrolyte / charge carrierWorking and features
AlkalineAqueous KOH or NaOH; hydroxide ionsOH⁻ moves from cathode toward anode. An established process; durability is useful, while liquid-electrolyte corrosion and current density can impose limitations.
PEMSolid proton-exchange polymer membrane; H⁺Protons pass through the membrane from anode to cathode. High-purity hydrogen; precious-metal catalysts and component cost are limitations.
Solid oxideSolid ceramic electrolyte; oxide ionsHigh-temperature steam electrolysis. Oxide ions travel toward the anode; high-temperature durability is a concern.

The electrolysis half-reactions are:

ElectrolyserCathodeAnode
Alkaline\(2H_2O+2e^-\rightarrow H_2+2OH^-\)\(2OH^-\rightarrow \dfrac{1}{2}O_2+H_2O+2e^-\)
PEM\(2H^++2e^-\rightarrow H_2\)\(H_2O\rightarrow \dfrac{1}{2}O_2+2H^++2e^-\)
Solid oxide\(H_2O+2e^-\rightarrow H_2+O^{2-}\)\(O^{2-}\rightarrow \dfrac{1}{2}O_2+2e^-\)

Hydrogen-production labels distinguish grey hydrogen from methane, brown hydrogen from coal, blue hydrogen with carbon capture, and turquoise hydrogen from methane pyrolysis. These labels describe production routes, not different hydrogen molecules.

Source discrepancy
The source says “Only green and blue hydrogen are reliably zero-carbon” while also describing incomplete carbon capture. Blue hydrogen is not automatically zero-carbon: its production starts with a carbon-containing feedstock and capture does not establish zero emissions.

Batteries: principle, features and classification

A battery stores energy in chemical form and supplies electrical energy through redox reactions. Useful features include compactness, suitable voltage stability, shelf life, energy efficiency and, for rechargeable batteries, cycle life.

Shelf life is the storage period over which a battery retains useful performance. Cycle life counts charge–discharge cycles under stated conditions.

\[\eta_{\rm energy}=\dfrac{\text{energy delivered on discharge}}{\text{energy supplied on charge}}\times100\%\]
TypeDefining featureExamples
PrimaryIntended for single use; not recharged in normal serviceDry cell; primary zinc–air cell
SecondaryCan be recharged by reversing the cell reactions with external electrical energyLead–acid and lithium-ion batteries
ReserveAn essential component is kept separate until activation, to permit long storageMagnesium–silver chloride and zinc–silver oxide systems
Fuel cellFuel and oxidant are supplied continuously from outside the cellHydrogen–oxygen PEM fuel cell

Primary cells are disposed of or recycled after discharge; secondary cells are recharged for reuse. Fuel cells keep operating while reactants are supplied, whereas a battery normally carries its active materials within the device.

Limits and pending checks+

“Rechargeable” in the source’s general list is a desired feature for secondary batteries, not a property of every battery.

Zinc–air battery: construction and working

A zinc–air cell uses zinc as the negative electrode and oxygen from ambient air as the cathodic reactant. It is an alkaline metal–air battery; the oxygen supply is not stored as a conventional solid cathode active material.

  • Anode: zinc powder mixed with aqueous KOH and a gelling agent.
  • Air cathode: porous carbon with an oxygen-reduction catalyst, supported on a conducting matrix. Hydrophobic, gas-permeable layers regulate access of air and electrolyte.
  • Electrolyte: approximately 30% KOH. A separator keeps the electrodes apart, and an opening admits air.

During discharge, oxygen reaches the cathode and is reduced in the alkaline electrolyte. Zinc is oxidised at the anode. A simplified reaction sequence is:

\[\mathrm{Zn\rightarrow Zn^{2+}+2e^-}\]
\[\mathrm{Zn^{2+}+2OH^-\rightarrow Zn(OH)_2\rightarrow ZnO+H_2O}\]
\[\dfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{O_2}+\mathrm{H_2O}+2e^-\rightarrow2\mathrm{OH^-}\]
\[\text{Overall: }\mathrm{Zn}+\dfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{O_2}\rightarrow\mathrm{ZnO}\]

Advantages include low cost, high energy density and long sealed shelf life. Limitations include restricted power output and a limited service life after the air seal has been opened.

Applications include hearing aids, medical devices, pagers, voice transmitters and small portable power supplies.

Lithium-ion battery: construction, discharge and charge

A lithium-ion battery is rechargeable and operates by transfer of lithium ions between its electrodes. In this example, the negative electrode is graphite and the positive electrode is lithium cobalt oxide. Lithium salts in a suitable non-aqueous electrolyte carry ions between the electrodes.

On discharge, lithium leaves the lithiated graphite negative electrode. Li⁺ travels through the electrolyte toward the positive electrode; electrons travel through the external load from the negative electrode to the positive electrode.

\[\text{Negative electrode: }\mathrm{LiC_6\rightarrow C_6+Li^++e^-}\]
\[\text{Positive electrode: }\mathrm{CoO_2+Li^++e^-\rightarrow LiCoO_2}\]
\[\text{Overall, simplified: }\mathrm{LiC_6+CoO_2\rightarrow C_6+LiCoO_2}\]

This idealised end-member reaction illustrates ion transfer. Charging supplies external electrical energy and reverses the transfer: lithium returns to graphite. Practical cells operate within controlled voltage and state-of-charge limits.

Source discrepancy
Page 9 says electrons move “in the opposite direction to the ions”. Its own half-reactions show that, during discharge, both go from the negative-electrode side toward the positive-electrode side, through different paths. The printed anode reaction also omits the + on Li; charge balance requires Li⁺.
  • Advantages: high energy density, low maintenance and relatively low self-discharge.
  • Limitations: overheating and fire risks, manufacturing cost, transport restrictions and the need to avoid abusive overcharge or overdischarge.
  • Applications: mobile phones, laptops, cameras, portable electronics and aerospace equipment.

Sodium-ion batteries and comparison with lithium-ion

A sodium-ion battery uses Na⁺ as its mobile ion. An example uses a layered transition-metal-oxide positive electrode, a hard-carbon negative electrode and NaPF₆ in carbonate solvents.

FeatureSodium-ion exampleLithium-ion example
Charge carrierNa⁺Li⁺
Negative electrodeHard carbonGraphite
Positive electrodeLayered transition-metal oxideLayered transition-metal oxide
Electrolyte exampleNaPF₆ in carbonate solventsLiPF₆ in non-aqueous carbonate solvents
Material availabilitySodium is abundantLithium supply is more geographically concentrated
Potential use highlightedGrid storage and cost-sensitive applicationsPortable electronics and electric vehicles

Advantages of sodium-ion technology include raw-material abundance, potential cost benefits and compatibility with aspects of existing battery manufacturing. Lithium-ion technology has an established manufacturing base and typically higher energy density.

The source quotes 2.3–3.3 V and 100–160 Wh/kg for Na-ion versus 3.2–4.2 V and 150–250 Wh/kg for Li-ion, without identifying cells or test conditions. These are source examples, not specifications for all cells. Absolute safety, cycle-life and environmental rankings require chemistry-specific evidence.

Proton-exchange membrane fuel cell

A PEM fuel cell converts the chemical energy of hydrogen and oxygen into electricity. Hydrogen and oxygen must be supplied continuously. It is also called a polymer-electrolyte membrane fuel cell.

  • Anode: porous carbon with platinum catalyst, supplied with hydrogen.
  • Cathode: porous carbon with platinum catalyst, supplied with oxygen or air.
  • Electrolyte: a proton-conducting polymer membrane, such as Nafion, which passes protons while preventing electrons from passing through the electrolyte.

Hydrogen splits at the anode into protons and electrons. Protons pass through the membrane. Electrons pass through the external circuit, delivering electrical power. At the cathode, oxygen combines with protons and electrons to form water.

\[\text{Anode: }2\mathrm{H_2}\rightarrow4\mathrm{H^+}+4e^-\]
\[\text{Cathode: }\mathrm{O_2}+4\mathrm{H^+}+4e^-\rightarrow2\mathrm{H_2O}\]
\[\text{Overall: }2\mathrm{H_2}+\mathrm{O_2}\rightarrow2\mathrm{H_2O}+\text{electrical energy}+\text{heat}\]

Typical operating conditions for this example are 60–80°C and 1–3 atm. Limitations include platinum cost, sensitivity to impurities such as carbon monoxide, and water management: too much water causes flooding, while too little dries the membrane.

Applications include fuel-cell vehicles, portable and backup power, and stationary generation. Water is the local reaction product; the complete environmental impact also depends on hydrogen production.
