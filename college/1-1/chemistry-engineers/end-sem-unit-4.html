Polymers and classification

A polymer is a macromolecular substance built from many repeating units derived from monomers. Polymerisation is the process by which monomer molecules form polymer molecules.

BasisClasses and examples
OriginNatural: starch, cellulose, proteins and natural rubber. Synthetic: polyethylene, PVC, PTFE and synthetic rubbers. Semisynthetic: chemically modified natural polymers such as cellulose derivatives.
Monomer speciesHomopolymers use one monomer species; copolymers use more than one.
Chain structureLinear chains; branched chains; three-dimensional cross-linked networks.
TacticityIsotactic: substituents have a regular same-side arrangement; syndiotactic: a regular alternating arrangement; atactic: an irregular arrangement.
Function / mechanical behaviourPlastics, fibres and elastomers.
SynthesisAddition and condensation polymerisation.

Linear chains can pack closely; branching changes packing and physical properties. Cross-links connect chains into a network. Properties depend on chain structure, chemistry and intermolecular forces.

Elastomers contain flexible chains and suitable cross-links, permitting large reversible deformation. Fibres are associated with strong intermolecular attractions and high tensile strength. Plastics can be shaped during manufacture and are used for their combination of processability, low mass and useful mechanical, thermal or electrical properties.

Limits and pending checks+

The notes give 600 as a universal degree-of-polymerisation boundary and list polypropylene’s pendant methyl group as proof of chain branching. These classifications require checking and are not used as general definitions.

Addition, condensation and copolymerisation

In these addition examples, unsaturated monomers add to a growing chain without elimination of small molecules. Ethene forms polyethylene:

\[n\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2}\rightarrow[\mathrm{-CH_2-CH_2-}]_n\]

In condensation polymerisation, functional groups react to form links between units, with elimination of a small molecule such as water or hydrogen chloride. An example is nylon-6,6 from a dicarboxylic acid and a diamine.

FeatureAddition examplesCondensation examples
MonomersTypically unsaturated molecules in the examples shownMolecules with reactive functional groups such as –OH, –COOH and –NH₂
Small-molecule eliminationNone in the illustrated addition reactionsWater, HCl or another small molecule is eliminated
GrowthChains grow at active sitesFunctional groups on molecules react stepwise
ExamplesPolyethylene, PVC and PTFENylon and phenol–formaldehyde resins

Copolymerisation uses more than one monomer species. An example is styrene–butadiene rubber, formed from styrene and 1,3-butadiene.

Source discrepancy
The source table equates addition with thermoplastics and condensation with thermosets. Polymerisation route and thermal behaviour are different classifications. The source’s own nylon-6,6 reaction shows a linear condensation polymer; condensation does not imply a cross-linked thermoset.

Teflon (PTFE): synthesis, properties and uses

PTFE is polytetrafluoroethylene, commonly known as Teflon. It is formed by addition polymerisation of tetrafluoroethylene. Polymerisation is carried out under pressure with a peroxide initiator.

\[n\mathrm{CF_2{=}CF_2}\rightarrow[\mathrm{-CF_2-CF_2-}]_n\]

A precursor route uses dechlorination of dichlorotetrafluoroethane to obtain tetrafluoroethylene.

  • Properties: chemical inertness, excellent electrical insulation, a non-stick surface and useful thermal resistance. A typical density is of 2.1–2.3 g/cm³.
  • Uses: wire and cable insulation, non-stick coatings, bearings, seals, stopcocks and electrical-component insulation.
Limits and pending checks+

The quoted 350°C “thermal resistance” is not adopted as a safe continuous-service temperature. A grade-specific material reference is needed for a design limit. The source’s causal explanation based solely on intermolecular attraction also needs checking. Structural drawings remain pending.

Buna-S: styrene–butadiene rubber

Buna-S is a copolymer of 1,3-butadiene and styrene. One formulation contains approximately 75% butadiene and 25% styrene, polymerised at about 50°C using a peroxide initiator.

The butadiene monomer is CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂; styrene is C₆H₅–CH=CH₂. Both contribute units to the copolymer chain.

  • Properties: abrasion resistance, load-bearing capability, elasticity and the ability to be vulcanised. It swells in organic solvents and is susceptible to ozone attack.
  • Applications: automobile tyres, shoe soles, gaskets, hoses, floor tiles, tank linings, adhesives and cable insulation.

Kevlar: aromatic polyamide fibre

Kevlar is an aromatic polyamide fibre. A simplified condensation scheme uses a para-substituted aromatic diamine (p-phenylenediamine) reacting with a para-substituted aromatic dicarboxylic acid (terephthalic acid), producing a chain with amide links. This simplified scheme does not specify industrial manufacturing conditions.

The repeating chain contains para-linked aromatic rings and –CO–NH– groups. Strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding contributes to high tensile strength and structural rigidity.

  • Properties: low mass, high tensile strength, low electrical conductivity, chemical resistance and flame resistance. Exposure to ultraviolet light can discolour and damage the fibre.
  • Uses: body armour and helmets, ropes and cables, belts and hoses, aircraft and boat composites, sporting goods, brake and clutch components and protective clothing.
Limits and pending checks+

The source’s “five times as strong as steel” needs a stated comparison basis. The displayed acid-based preparation and the detailed structural drawing need checking against the prescribed textbook before being used as a complete synthesis answer.

Conducting polymers: types and doping

Conducting polymers can carry electrical current. Examples are polyacetylene, polyaniline and polypyrrole. Their useful features include low mass, flexibility and the ability to combine electrical and polymer-processing properties.

Intrinsically conducting polymers have a conjugated backbone. Conductivity depends on factors including conjugation, doping, temperature and measurement conditions.

TypeExplanation
Conjugated backboneDelocalised π-electron systems provide the electronic structure needed for conduction.
p-type dopingOxidation removes electrons and creates positive charge carriers on the backbone; iodine treatment is an example.
n-type dopingReduction introduces electrons and negative charge carriers; sodium naphthalenide is an example.
Conducting-filler compositesA polymer binder holds conducting carbon or metal fillers. Conductivity depends on forming connected conducting paths.
BlendsA conventional polymer is blended with a conducting polymer to combine useful properties.

The percolation threshold is the minimum filler concentration at which a connected conducting network produces substantial conduction in the composite.

Applications include rechargeable batteries, electrochromic displays and optical filters, chemical sensors, flexible electronics, circuit components, photovoltaic devices, corrosion protection and controlled drug-release systems.

Fibre-reinforced plastics (FRPs)

Additional explanation · PankusDeskExplain the roles of fibre and matrix separately+

The fibres carry load and provide much of the composite stiffness and strength. The matrix holds the fibres in position and alignment, protects them during manufacture and use, and distributes load among them. These complementary roles explain why an FRP is described as a composite rather than simply a plastic containing loose fibres.

The supporting slides identify carbon-, glass- and aramid-fibre systems and show structural retrofitting applications. Use those examples to explain classification and applications; detailed manufacturing or structural-design procedures are supplementary to the current Chemistry syllabus.

Based on 6 frp.pdf · PDF pages 2, 3, 14, 15. Source and remaining checks. Existing answers and figures are unchanged.

An FRP is a composite with a polymer matrix reinforced by fibres. Common reinforcing fibres include glass, carbon and aramid. The matrix binds and supports the reinforcement, while fibre type, orientation and distribution affect the composite’s properties.

ReinforcementFeatures
Glass fibreRelatively low cost and mass, useful tensile strength and chemical/corrosion resistance; moisture can reduce strength.
Carbon fibreHigh strength and stiffness, including useful performance at elevated temperatures and resistance to many chemicals.
Aramid fibreLightweight aromatic polyamide fibres such as Kevlar and Nomex; ultraviolet exposure can affect appearance and mechanical properties.

Fibre forms include parallel continuous fibres, randomly distributed short chopped fibres and woven fabrics.

  • Features: high strength and stiffness relative to mass, fatigue and corrosion resistance, design flexibility and repair possibilities.
  • Applications: vehicle bodywork and racing components; boat hulls and shafts; aircraft and rocket components; sporting goods and musical instruments; tanks, safety equipment and circuit boards.
Limits and pending checks+

Statements that every FRP is stronger, cheaper or more durable than steel or aluminium are not universal material specifications.

Shape-memory polymers: activation and applications

Shape-memory polymers can recover a programmed permanent shape from a temporary deformed shape when an appropriate stimulus is applied. They can be classified by activation method.

ActivationMechanism described
ThermalA temperature change activates recovery.
ElectricalConducting fillers allow current to produce heat, which triggers shape recovery.
LightPhotosensitive groups act as molecular switches.
MagneticMagnetic particles produce heating in an alternating magnetic field.
WaterWater changes the relevant transition behaviour and permits shape recovery in a suitably programmed polymer.

For example, in a programmed Z-shaped rod, the part immersed in water recovers toward its permanent straight form as water changes its transition behaviour.

  • Textiles: responsive garments, crease retention and safety fabrics.
  • Biomedical devices: responsive drug delivery, tissue scaffolds, catheters, stents and customised devices.
  • Electronics and industry: actuators, disassembly fasteners, sealing, packaging and damping elements.
  • Robotics: shape-adaptive grips and foams.
Limits and pending checks+

The source’s below-glass-transition programming statement and universal claims of biodegradability/biocompatibility need qualification. They are not adopted as properties of every SMP. The programming/recovery drawing remains pending.

Biodegradable polymers and polylactic acid (PLA)

Biodegradable polymers can be broken down by biological activity under suitable environmental conditions. Degradation products and rates depend on the polymer and environment. They are studied as alternatives to persistent plastics and as materials for selected biomedical applications.

Natural examples include starch, cellulose, chitosan, alginate, gelatin, collagen and nanocellulose. Synthetic examples include PLA, PGA, PCL and PBS.

PLA is a biobased aliphatic polyester derived from lactic acid. Production begins with fermentation of carbohydrate feedstocks to lactic acid, followed by condensation polymerisation or ring-opening polymerisation of lactide.

\[\text{PLA repeating unit: }[\mathrm{-O-CH(CH_3)-CO-}]_n\]
  • Features: renewable feedstock, transparency, useful mechanical properties and processability by moulding, extrusion and fibre spinning.
  • Applications: packaging, cups, trays and films; selected sutures and drug-delivery systems; tissue scaffolds; fibres and 3D-printing filament.
  • Limitations: brittleness, moisture and thermal limitations, and slow degradation in ordinary soil or marine conditions.

Industrial composting conditions are important for PLA degradation. Biobased origin does not mean that a finished article will rapidly degrade in any environment.

Limits and pending checks+

Claims of complete decomposition or safety for every biomedical/food-contact use require conditions and a suitable material grade. Nanocellulose is named in the source syllabus, but has no separate explanatory treatment in this packet.
