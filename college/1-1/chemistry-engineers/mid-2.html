Unit 3 · Choose Q1 OR Q2

Part B · Q1 · 4 marksDescribe green hydrogen and explain the generation of hydrogen by Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) electrolysis method.+

Green hydrogen is hydrogen produced by electrolysis of water using electricity from renewable sources such as solar and wind energy.

Construction: a proton-exchange membrane separates the anode and cathode. Water is supplied to the anode, and the electrodes are connected to a renewable-powered DC supply.

Working

  • At the positive anode, water splits into oxygen, hydrogen ions and electrons.
  • Hydrogen ions pass through the membrane to the negative cathode.
  • The external DC circuit supplies electrons to the cathode, where hydrogen gas is formed.

Anode

\[\mathrm{H_2O\rightarrow\dfrac12 O_2+2H^++2e^-}\]

Cathode

\[\mathrm{2H^++2e^-\rightarrow H_2}\]

Overall reaction

\[\boxed{\mathrm{2H_2O\rightarrow2H_2+O_2}}\]

The solid polymer membrane conducts protons and keeps the product gases separated. PEM electrolysers give high-purity hydrogen and use catalytic electrodes. The electricity must come from renewable sources for the hydrogen to be called green.

Unit 4 · Choose Q3 OR Q4

Part B · Q3 · 8 marks
(a) Define conducting polymers. Explain their classification and write at least two important applications. [4 marks]
(b) Define Shape Memory Polymers (SMPs). Classify them and explain their applications with suitable examples. [4 marks]+

(a) Conducting polymers

Conducting polymers conduct electricity through their conjugated backbone or incorporated conducting materials.

Classification

  • Intrinsically conducting polymers: possess conjugated double bonds. Examples: polyacetylene, polyaniline and polypyrrole. Doping increases conductivity.
  • p-type doping removes electrons; n-type doping adds electrons.
  • Extrinsically conducting polymers: insulating polymers made conducting by adding carbon, metal particles or conducting polymers.

In intrinsically conducting polymers, overlapping p-orbitals along the conjugated chain permit electron delocalisation. Doping creates additional mobile charge carriers. In extrinsic materials, conducting fillers or conducting-polymer blends provide connected paths for current.

Applications: antistatic coatings, sensors, battery electrodes, electrochromic displays and flexible electronic devices.

(b) Shape-memory polymers

Shape-memory polymers retain a temporary shape and recover their permanent shape when exposed to a suitable stimulus.

Working: heat above the transition temperature, deform and cool under constraint to fix the temporary shape. Reheating restores the permanent shape.

Class / stimulusExample or application
Thermally activatedSelf-deploying structures and actuators
Electrically activatedConducting-filler composites for actuators and robotic grippers
Magnetically activatedMagnetic-particle composites heated by a magnetic field
Light activatedLight-responsive actuators
Water activatedA programmed bent rod recovering its permanent shape in water

Permanent cross-links or netpoints retain the original shape. Switching segments fix the temporary shape on cooling and regain mobility on stimulation, allowing recovery. Electrical and magnetic activation often work by heating the polymer through conducting fillers or magnetic particles.

Suitable biomedical SMPs are used or investigated for catheters, stents and drug-delivery devices.

Part B · Q4 · 8 marks
(a) Explain the synthesis, properties, and applications of Kevlar polymer. [4 marks]
(b) What are biodegradable polymers? Give their important features and outline the applications of Polylactic Acid (PLA). [4 marks]+

(a) Kevlar

Kevlar is an aromatic polyamide prepared by condensation of p-phenylenediamine with terephthalic acid in the simplified reaction scheme, with elimination of water.

Repeating unit

\[\mathrm{[{-}NH{-}C_6H_4{-}NH{-}CO{-}C_6H_4{-}CO{-}]_n}\]

Properties

  • High tensile strength and stiffness with low weight.
  • Good heat and flame resistance.
  • Strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding.

Its rigid, para-linked aromatic chains can align closely. Hydrogen bonding between amide groups holds adjacent chains together, giving high tensile strength relative to weight. This combination explains its use in protective clothing and reinforcement.

Applications: bullet-resistant jackets, helmets, ropes, tyre reinforcement, protective clothing and aerospace composites.

(b) Biodegradable polymers and PLA

Biodegradable polymers break down through biological activity under suitable environmental conditions.

Features

  • Reduce accumulation of persistent polymer waste.
  • Contain linkages that can undergo degradation.
  • Suitable grades can form temporary biomedical products.
  • Degradation depends on moisture, temperature and biological conditions.

Polylactic acid (PLA) is a biodegradable polyester made from lactic acid obtained by fermentation of carbohydrates.

Repeating unit

\[\mathrm{[{-}O{-}CH(CH_3){-}CO{-}]_n}\]

PLA contains ester linkages that can undergo hydrolysis during degradation. It is a thermoplastic with useful stiffness and transparency. Suitable composting conditions are important; it does not disappear rapidly in every environment. Biomedical grades are useful where gradual resorption is required.

Applications: packaging films, disposable cups, 3D-printing filament, surgical sutures, drug-delivery systems and tissue scaffolds.

Unit 5 · Choose Q5 OR Q6

Part B · Q5 · 8 marks
(a) What are nanomaterials? Explain the characterization of nanomaterials using Scanning Tunnelling Microscopy (STM). [4 marks]
(b) Explain how UV-Visible spectroscopy is useful in the analysis of dye pollutants in industrial effluents by taking Methylene blue as an example. [4 marks]+

(a) Nanomaterials and STM

Nanomaterials have at least one dimension approximately between 1 and 100 nm.

Principle: STM works on quantum tunnelling of electrons between a sharp conducting tip and a conducting sample.

Working

  • Bring the tip very close to the sample and apply a small bias to produce a tunnelling current.
  • Move the tip across the surface using a piezoelectric scanner.
  • In constant-current mode, feedback adjusts the tip height to maintain constant current.
  • Record the height changes to obtain a surface image.

The main components are a sharp conducting tip, bias supply, piezoelectric scanner, current detector, feedback controller and computer. Tunnelling current changes very strongly with tip–sample separation, making small surface-height differences detectable. Constant-height mode instead records current changes while scanning at a fixed nominal tip height.

Uses: studying surface atoms, defects and nanostructures. The sample must provide a conducting path.

(b) Dye analysis by UV–Visible spectroscopy

Principle: methylene blue absorbs visible light. Within the working range, absorbance follows Beer–Lambert law.

Beer–Lambert law

\[A=\varepsilon lc\]

Procedure

  • Prepare methylene-blue standards of known concentrations.
  • Set the instrument at the absorption maximum, approximately 664 nm in dilute aqueous solution.
  • Use a solvent blank and measure the absorbance of each standard.
  • Plot absorbance against concentration to obtain a calibration graph.
  • Measure the effluent sample under the same conditions.
  • Read its concentration from the calibration graph and apply any dilution factor.

Treatment efficiency

\[\%\text{ dye removal}=\dfrac{C_i-C_f}{C_i}\times100\]

In A = εlc, ε is molar absorptivity, l is the optical path length and c is concentration. Use the same cuvette and conditions for standards and sample. Dilute samples outside the calibration range; turbidity and other absorbing substances can interfere.

UV–Visible spectroscopy is therefore useful for measuring dye concentration and treatment efficiency.

Part B · Q6 · 8 marks
(a) What are self-healing materials? Explain the working principle of self-healing materials with suitable examples. [4 marks]
(b) Explain the working principle and applications of an amperometric glucose biosensor. [4 marks]+

(a) Self-healing materials

Self-healing materials possess a built-in ability to repair damage and restore their performance.

Working principle

  • Triggering: damage activates the healing mechanism.
  • Transportation: healing material moves into the damaged region.
  • Chemical repair: polymerisation or bond re-formation closes the crack.

In a microcapsule system, a crack breaks embedded capsules. The healing agent flows into the crack and reacts to form a solid repair. In intrinsic systems, reversible bonds reconnect, often with heating.

Capsule-based and vascular systems are extrinsic: healing material is stored in capsules or channels within the matrix. In intrinsic systems, repair arises from reversible bonds or interactions in the polymer itself. Healing can be autonomic, triggered by damage, or activated externally by heat or another stimulus.

Examples: protective coatings, polymer composites, concrete and scratch-repair coatings.

(b) Amperometric glucose biosensor

An amperometric glucose biosensor measures the current produced by an enzyme-catalysed reaction.

Construction: glucose oxidase is immobilised near a working electrode. A reference electrode and current-measuring electronics complete the measuring system.

Enzyme reaction

\[\mathrm{Glucose+O_2\xrightarrow{glucose\ oxidase}Gluconolactone+H_2O_2}\]

Gluconolactone reacts with water to form gluconic acid.

Detecting-electrode reaction

\[\mathrm{H_2O_2\rightarrow O_2+2H^++2e^-}\]

The measured current is related to glucose concentration by calibration.

A suitable potential is maintained at the working electrode. Hydrogen peroxide formed by the enzyme reaction is oxidised there, releasing electrons. Over the calibrated working range, a greater glucose concentration produces a greater reaction-related current. The enzyme provides selectivity; the electrode converts the chemical change into an electrical signal.

Applications: blood-glucose meters, continuous glucose monitoring, and glucose analysis in food and fermentation processes.

Part B · Q1 · 4 marksWhat is knocking of an IC engine? Mention its ill effects & how can it be prevented?+

Knocking in a petrol engine is sudden autoignition of unburnt fuel–air mixture ahead of the normal flame, producing pressure waves and a metallic sound.

Ill effects

  • Noise and vibration.
  • Loss of power and efficiency.
  • Overheating and damage to pistons and other parts.

Prevention

  • Use fuel of suitable high octane number.
  • Maintain correct ignition timing and avoid excessive compression ratio.
  • Provide effective cooling and prevent carbon deposits.

In a diesel engine, knocking results from excessive ignition delay. Suitable cetane quality, correct injection timing and good atomisation help prevent it.

Petrol-engine knock is associated with premature autoignition of the end-gas, whereas diesel knock follows delayed ignition and rapid burning of accumulated fuel. Hence octane quality is relevant to petrol engines and cetane quality to diesel engines.

Unit 4 · Choose Q3 OR Q4

Part B · Q3 · 8 marks
(a) What is cathodic protection? Explain about impressed current cathodic protection. [4 marks]
(b) Passivity of a metal leads to more corrosion resistance. Why? [4 marks]+

(a) Impressed-current cathodic protection

Cathodic protection prevents corrosion by making the metal structure the cathode.

Impressed-current method

  • Connect the structure to the negative terminal of a DC supply.
  • Connect a suitable auxiliary anode to the positive terminal and place both in contact with the electrolyte.
  • The supply delivers electrons to the structure, suppressing metal dissolution.
  • Oxidation occurs at the auxiliary anode. Adjust the protective current to suit the structure.

The structure receives electrons and supports a reduction reaction, such as oxygen reduction in aerated water, instead of losing iron by oxidation. The auxiliary anode supports the balancing oxidation. The method suits large structures, but needs continuous power and monitoring to maintain the required protective current.

Applications: long pipelines, storage tanks and marine structures.

(b) Passivity

Passivity is the condition in which a metal shows much greater corrosion resistance than expected from its position in the galvanic series.

Reason

  • A thin, adherent and relatively non-porous oxide film forms on the surface.
  • The film separates the metal from the corrosive environment.
  • It restricts transport of reacting species and reduces further dissolution.
  • In suitable oxidising conditions, the film can re-form after minor damage.

Examples: Al₂O₃ on aluminium and chromium-rich oxide on stainless steel. Chlorides or unsuitable conditions can break down the film and cause local attack.

The protective film must remain continuous and adherent. Ordinary iron rust is porous and does not give equivalent protection. Hence passivity depends on both the metal and its environment, rather than on the metal’s position in the galvanic series alone.

Part B · Q4 · 8 marksExplain the mechanism involved in the rusting of iron based on wet theory of corrosion.+

Wet or electrochemical corrosion occurs when a metal contacts moisture or an electrolyte. Anodic and cathodic regions develop on the surface. Metal dissolves at the anode, electrons travel through the metal, and reduction occurs at the cathode.

Anodic dissolution

\[\mathrm{Fe\rightarrow Fe^{2+}+2e^-}\]

Small surface differences, impurities or unequal oxygen concentration establish local electrochemical cells. An electrical path through the metal and an ionic path through the electrolyte complete the circuit. Only the anodic areas lose iron directly.

Hydrogen-evolution mechanism: in an acidic, poorly aerated medium, hydrogen ions accept electrons at the cathode.

Cathodic reaction

\[\mathrm{2H^++2e^-\rightarrow H_2\uparrow}\]

Overall acid-corrosion reaction

\[\mathrm{Fe+2H^+\rightarrow Fe^{2+}+H_2\uparrow}\]

Hydrogen ions are consumed at the cathode and hydrogen bubbles escape, while ferrous ions enter the solution at the anode. The cathodic process is therefore hydrogen evolution rather than rust formation itself.

Oxygen-absorption mechanism: in aerated neutral or alkaline water, dissolved oxygen accepts electrons at cathodic regions.

Cathodic reaction

\[\mathrm{O_2+2H_2O+4e^-\rightarrow4OH^-}\]

Ferrous hydroxide formation

\[\mathrm{Fe^{2+}+2OH^-\rightarrow Fe(OH)_2}\]

Further oxidation

\[\mathrm{4Fe(OH)_2+O_2+2H_2O\rightarrow4Fe(OH)_3}\]

The oxygen-poor region usually acts as the anode, while the better-aerated region supports oxygen reduction. Ferrous ions and hydroxide ions meet in the electrolyte to form ferrous hydroxide, which is further oxidised in the presence of oxygen and water.

Ferric hydroxide undergoes dehydration and ageing to form hydrated ferric oxide, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (rust). Rust is porous and non-protective, allowing corrosion to continue.

Unit 5 · Choose Q5 OR Q6

Part B · Q5 · 8 marks
(a) Discuss the construction and working principle of a typical biosensors? [4 marks]
(b) Explain the mechanism involved in equipment, exposed to extreme operational conditions like hypoid gears. [4 marks]+

(a) Biosensor

A biosensor combines a biological recognition element with a transducer to detect an analyte.

Construction

  • Bioreceptor: enzyme, antibody, cell or nucleic acid that recognises the analyte.
  • Transducer: converts the biological response into a measurable signal.
  • Signal processor: amplifies and processes the signal.
  • Display: shows the calibrated result.

Working: the analyte interacts with the bioreceptor, producing a change in current, potential, heat, light or mass. The transducer detects this change; the processed signal gives analyte concentration.

The biological element is immobilised on a support close to the transducer so that it remains active and its response can be detected efficiently. A known-concentration standard is used for calibration. Selectivity, sensitivity, rapid response and reproducibility are important characteristics.

Example: glucose oxidase in a glucose biosensor.

(b) Extreme-pressure lubrication

Hypoid gears operate under high load and considerable sliding, producing high local temperatures. They require extreme-pressure lubrication.

Mechanism

  • Severe load and temperature can break the ordinary lubricant film.
  • Suitable sulphur- or phosphorus-containing additives react with the hot metal surface.
  • Protective films, such as metal sulphides or phosphates, form at contact points.
  • These films shear more readily than the metal, preventing welding, scoring and seizure.

The additives react mainly at hot contact points to form an adherent, low-shear layer. This sacrificial layer prevents direct metallic contact when ordinary oil or boundary films fail. The additive must be compatible with the contacting metals and operating temperature.

Applications: hypoid gears, heavily loaded gear systems and severe metal-forming operations.

Part B · Q4 · 8 marksWhat is differential aeration corrosion? Write a note on the following.
(a) Water line corrosion
(b) pitting corrosion+

(a) Waterline corrosion

Differential-aeration corrosion occurs when different regions of a metal receive unequal oxygen. The less-aerated region becomes the anode and corrodes; the more-aerated region becomes the cathode.

Waterline corrosion

  • In a partly filled steel tank, water near the surface contains more dissolved oxygen.
  • The less-aerated region just below the waterline acts as the anode.
  • The oxygen-rich region acts as the cathode.
  • Corrosion is concentrated below the waterline.

Anode

\[\mathrm{Fe\rightarrow Fe^{2+}+2e^-}\]

Cathode

\[\mathrm{O_2+2H_2O+4e^-\rightarrow4OH^-}\]

Water below the surface is replenished with oxygen less readily than water near the air–water interface. The resulting concentration cell can form on one piece of steel without contact with a second metal. Ferrous ions from the anodic region combine with hydroxide and ultimately form rust near the attacked zone.

(b) Pitting corrosion

Pitting is highly localised corrosion producing small holes or pits.

Mechanism

  • A protective film breaks down at a small region.
  • The exposed region becomes a small anode; the large surrounding surface acts as the cathode.
  • High anodic current density causes rapid local penetration.
  • Restricted oxygen supply and aggressive ions inside the pit help sustain attack.

Dust or another deposit can shield a small region from oxygen and initiate local attack. Once a pit develops, the small anodic area is coupled to a much larger cathodic surface. Acidification and chloride accumulation inside the pit can accelerate its growth, even while most of the surrounding surface remains intact.

Pitting is dangerous because deep penetration can occur with little overall metal loss.

Part B · Q5 · 8 marksExplain the following properties of lubricants and give their significance?
(a) Cloud point
(b) Pour point
(c) Flash point
(d) Fire point+

(a) Cloud point

Temperature at which oil first becomes cloudy on cooling due to wax separation.

Significance: Indicates wax deposition and possible filter blockage in cold service.

Wax crystals can obstruct filters and narrow oil passages before the oil completely loses its ability to flow. Select an oil with a cloud point suitably below the intended cold-service conditions.

(b) Pour point

Lowest temperature at which oil flows under the specified test conditions.

Significance: A low pour point is needed for cold starting and pumpability.

An oil that cannot flow readily may fail to reach bearings during cold starting. Pour point is generally lower than cloud point; it is a test-based flow property, not simply the temperature at which the oil first becomes cloudy.

(c) Flash point

Lowest specified-test temperature at which vapour ignites momentarily.

Significance: Indicates volatility and fire risk; a low value may suggest volatile contamination.

At the flash point, sufficient vapour is present for a brief flash, but burning is not sustained. A lubricant should have a flash point suitably above its operating temperature; an unexpectedly low value may indicate contamination with a volatile fuel.

(d) Fire point

Temperature at which vapour supports sustained burning under the test conditions.

Significance: Helps assess fire risk during high-temperature operation.

At this temperature, vapour is generated rapidly enough to maintain combustion after ignition. Fire point is normally higher than flash point. Both properties help in selecting and safely handling lubricants for elevated-temperature service.

Part B · Q2 · 4 marksExplain working of methanol-oxygen fuel cell with chemical equations.+

A methanol–oxygen fuel cell converts the chemical energy of methanol and oxygen directly into electrical energy.

Construction: two catalytic electrodes are separated by a proton-exchange membrane such as Nafion. The anode contains platinum–ruthenium catalyst and the cathode contains platinum catalyst.

Working

  • Aqueous methanol is supplied to the anode, where it is oxidised to carbon dioxide, protons and electrons.
  • Protons pass through the membrane to the cathode.
  • Electrons pass through the external circuit and produce current.
  • Oxygen supplied to the cathode combines with protons and electrons to form water.

Anode

\[\mathrm{CH_3OH+H_2O\rightarrow CO_2+6H^++6e^-}\]

Cathode

\[\mathrm{\dfrac32O_2+6H^++6e^-\rightarrow3H_2O}\]

Overall

\[\boxed{\mathrm{CH_3OH+\dfrac32O_2\rightarrow CO_2+2H_2O}}\]

The membrane conducts protons but does not provide the external electron path. Continuous supply of methanol and oxygen maintains operation, while carbon dioxide and water are removed. The fuel cell therefore produces electricity directly without first converting the fuel energy into mechanical work.

Unit 4 · Choose Q3 OR Q4

Part B · Q3 · 8 marksDiscuss in detail the mechanism of electrochemical corrosion theory with the evolution of hydrogen and absorption of oxygen with chemical equations.+

Wet or electrochemical corrosion occurs when a metal contacts moisture or an electrolyte. Anodic and cathodic regions develop on the surface. Metal dissolves at the anode, electrons travel through the metal, and reduction occurs at the cathode.

Anodic dissolution

\[\mathrm{Fe\rightarrow Fe^{2+}+2e^-}\]

Small surface differences, impurities or unequal oxygen concentration establish local electrochemical cells. An electrical path through the metal and an ionic path through the electrolyte complete the circuit. Only the anodic areas lose iron directly.

Hydrogen-evolution mechanism: in an acidic, poorly aerated medium, hydrogen ions accept electrons at the cathode.

Cathodic reaction

\[\mathrm{2H^++2e^-\rightarrow H_2\uparrow}\]

Overall acid-corrosion reaction

\[\mathrm{Fe+2H^+\rightarrow Fe^{2+}+H_2\uparrow}\]

Hydrogen ions are consumed at the cathode and hydrogen bubbles escape, while ferrous ions enter the solution at the anode. The cathodic process is therefore hydrogen evolution rather than rust formation itself.

Oxygen-absorption mechanism: in aerated neutral or alkaline water, dissolved oxygen accepts electrons at cathodic regions.

Cathodic reaction

\[\mathrm{O_2+2H_2O+4e^-\rightarrow4OH^-}\]

Ferrous hydroxide formation

\[\mathrm{Fe^{2+}+2OH^-\rightarrow Fe(OH)_2}\]

Further oxidation

\[\mathrm{4Fe(OH)_2+O_2+2H_2O\rightarrow4Fe(OH)_3}\]

The oxygen-poor region usually acts as the anode, while the better-aerated region supports oxygen reduction. Ferrous ions and hydroxide ions meet in the electrolyte to form ferrous hydroxide, which is further oxidised in the presence of oxygen and water.

Ferric hydroxide undergoes dehydration and ageing to form hydrated ferric oxide, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (rust). Rust is porous and non-protective, allowing corrosion to continue.

(b) Sacrificial-anode protection

Cathodic protection prevents corrosion by making the metal structure the cathode of an electrochemical cell.

Sacrificial-anode method

  • Connect the iron/steel structure to a more active metal such as magnesium, zinc or aluminium in the same electrolyte.
  • The active metal becomes the anode and oxidises, supplying electrons to the structure.
  • The structure becomes cathodic and is protected from dissolution.
  • Inspect and replace the sacrificial anode as it is consumed. No external DC supply is needed.

Example: zinc anode

\[\mathrm{Zn\rightarrow Zn^{2+}+2e^-}\]

Electrons supplied by the active metal maintain the steel at a cathodic potential. In aerated water, reduction of dissolved oxygen can occur on the protected steel. Effective protection requires good electrical contact, a common electrolyte and enough sacrificial metal to supply the protective current.

Applications: underground pipelines, ship hulls, storage tanks and water heaters.

Part B · Q6 · 8 marksExplain in detail the following mechanisms of lubrication
(a) Thick film lubrication
(b) Boundary lubrication/thin film lubrication+

(a) Thick-film lubrication

Thick-film or hydrodynamic lubrication uses a continuous liquid film to separate the moving surfaces.

Mechanism

  • Lubricant fills surface irregularities and forms a continuous separating film.
  • Relative motion draws oil into the gap, developing pressure that supports the load.
  • Direct metal-to-metal contact is prevented.
  • Friction occurs mainly between layers of lubricant, reducing wear.

In a journal bearing, the moving shaft draws oil into a converging clearance. The resulting pressure supports the shaft and keeps surface asperities apart. A film thicker than the surface irregularities is required; too little oil or unsuitable viscosity can destroy this separation.

Conditions: adequate speed and low-to-moderate load. The oil must have suitable viscosity and a continuous supply.

Example: a shaft running in a well-lubricated bearing; delicate machinery such as watches, clocks and sewing machines.

(b) Boundary lubrication

Boundary or thin-film lubrication occurs when a complete thick film cannot be maintained, particularly at low speed, high load, or during starting and stopping.

Mechanism

  • Polar lubricant molecules attach to the metal surface by physical or chemical adsorption.
  • Their polar ends attach to the metal and hydrocarbon chains extend outward.
  • These adherent thin layers reduce direct metal contact.
  • Sliding occurs between the lubricant layers, reducing friction, wear and seizure.

Unlike thick-film lubrication, the load is not carried entirely by a continuous fluid film. Protection depends mainly on the oiliness and adhesion of the adsorbed molecular layers. These layers resist removal under load, while their outer portions slide relatively easily over one another.

Requirements: high oiliness and strong adhesion. Fatty oils and suitably formulated blended oils provide boundary lubrication.

Examples: heavily loaded, slowly moving bearings and machinery during startup/shutdown.
