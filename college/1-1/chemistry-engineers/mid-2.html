Part A · (b) · 2 marksDefine polymers. Give any two methods of classification of polymers.+
PankusDesk-authored solution

Polymers are high-molecular-mass macromolecules made of many repeating structural units derived from monomers. Two classification bases are: (1) origin—natural (cellulose), semi-synthetic (cellulose acetate) and synthetic (polyethylene); (2) monomer composition—homopolymers from one monomer species (polyethylene) and copolymers from two or more species (Buna-S). These are two independent methods of classification.

Part A · (d) · 2 marksWrite about the role of IR spectroscopy in night-vision security systems.+
PankusDesk-authored solution

Objects emit infrared radiation according to their temperature. A passive thermal night-vision camera detects this radiation and converts differences into an image, allowing people or intruders to be detected in darkness. Active near-IR systems instead illuminate the scene and detect reflected IR. This is an IR imaging/detection application; a night-vision camera need not be a molecular absorption spectrometer.

Original Unit 3 · Choose Q1 OR Q2

Part B · Q1 · 4 marksDescribe green hydrogen and explain the generation of hydrogen by Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) electrolysis method.+
PankusDesk-authored solution

Green hydrogen is hydrogen produced by water electrolysis using renewable electricity, such as solar or wind power. The renewable electricity requirement distinguishes green hydrogen from electrolysis powered by fossil-derived electricity.

Construction: a proton-conducting polymer membrane separates catalyst-coated anode and cathode. Water is supplied to the anode and a renewable-powered DC source provides the energy needed to split water. The membrane conducts H⁺, separates product gases and prevents electronic short-circuiting.

At the positive anode: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻. Protons cross the membrane from anode to cathode; the DC circuit removes electrons from the anode and supplies them to the cathode. At the negative cathode: 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂. Collect hydrogen and oxygen separately. Overall: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂.

PEM electrolysis consumes electrical energy to make hydrogen. It must not be confused with a hydrogen–oxygen PEM fuel cell, which consumes hydrogen and oxygen to generate electrical energy.

Part B · Q2 · 4 marksWith a neat diagram, explain the construction and working of a Lithium-ion battery.+
PankusDesk-authored solution

A lithium-ion cell shuttles Li⁺ reversibly between two intercalation electrodes. The supplied example uses graphite at the negative electrode, lithium cobalt oxide at the positive electrode, a separator and a non-aqueous lithium-salt electrolyte.

On discharge, the negative electrode releases lithium ions and electrons: LiC₆ → C₆ + Li⁺ + e⁻. The positive-electrode idealised reaction is CoO₂ + Li⁺ + e⁻ → LiCoO₂. Overall: LiC₆ + CoO₂ → C₆ + LiCoO₂. Li⁺ travels through the electrolyte and electrons through the external load toward the positive electrode.

Charging supplies external energy and reverses these transfers. The equations are idealised end-member bookkeeping; practical cells cycle over controlled partial states of charge, not complete conversion to pure CoO₂. Advantages include high energy density, relatively low self-discharge and long useful cycle life. Limitations include ageing, thermal-runaway risk and protection/charging requirements. Applications: phones, laptops, cameras, electric vehicles and storage.

Source diagram correction.

The charging panel puts “Current” and “Electron” labels on a shared dotted path. Do not read these as flows in the same direction: conventional current is opposite electron flow. During charging the external supply removes electrons from the positive electrode and delivers electrons to the negative electrode; Li⁺ moves through the electrolyte from positive to negative. During discharge, Li⁺ and electrons both move from negative to positive, through electrolyte and external load respectively. Use these directions and the written reactions when reproducing the source diagram.

Original Unit 4 · Choose Q3 OR Q4

Part B · Q3 · 8 marks
(a) Define conducting polymers. Explain their classification and write at least two important applications. [4 marks]
(b) Define Shape Memory Polymers (SMPs). Classify them and explain their applications with suitable examples. [4 marks]+
PankusDesk-authored solution

(a) Define conducting polymers. Explain their classification and write at least two important applications.

Conducting polymers carry charge through a conjugated polymer backbone or through conducting constituents in a composite. Intrinsic examples include polyacetylene, polyaniline and polypyrrole. Alternating/conjugated bonds permit delocalisation; doping creates additional mobile charge carriers. Oxidative (p-type) doping removes electrons, for example iodine treatment; reductive (n-type) doping adds electrons, for example with sodium naphthalenide.

Extrinsically conducting materials include polymers filled with carbon/metal particles and blends with conducting polymers. A connected conducting path forms above a composition-dependent percolation threshold. Conductivity depends on doping, structure, morphology and temperature; conjugation alone does not guarantee metal-like conduction.

Redox/doping changes can reversibly change optical absorption/colour, enabling electrochromic displays and optical filters. Other applications include antistatic coatings, sensors, flexible electronics, battery electrodes, corrosion-protection coatings and photovoltaic devices.

Answer basis: 09 Polymers.pdf · pages 9, 10, 11.

(b) Define Shape Memory Polymers (SMPs). Classify them and explain their applications with suitable examples.

A shape-memory polymer (SMP) can be programmed into a temporary shape and recover a permanent shape when stimulated. Permanent netpoints/cross-links preserve the reference shape; switching segments fix and release the temporary shape through a transition such as glass transition or melting.

Thermal cycle: heat above the switching transition, deform to a temporary shape, cool under constraint to fix it, then remove the constraint. Reheat to activate segmental mobility and recover the permanent shape. Unlike ordinary plastic deformation, the temporary deformation is deliberately recoverable.

Other activation routes include light-responsive chemistry, electrical/Joule heating with conducting fillers, magnetic heating of embedded particles and water-induced plasticisation. Electrical/magnetic activation may act through heat rather than a direct shape-changing field effect.

Features: lightweight, large programmable deformation, tunable switching temperature and flexible manufacture. Applications: actuators, self-deploying structures, seals, responsive textiles, robotics, catheters/stents and drug-delivery/scaffold research. Biocompatibility and biodegradability depend on the particular polymer and are not properties of all SMPs.

Classification by activating stimulus: thermally responsive, light-responsive, electrically activated, magnetically activated and water-responsive SMPs. Thermally programmed polymers can form deployable actuators; conducting-filler composites can recover through Joule heating; magnetic-particle composites recover under magnetic heating. The supplied water-responsive example is a programmed Z-shaped rod returning towards its straight permanent shape in water. Responsive catheters/stents and robotic grippers are further application examples; suitability depends on the formulation.

Answer basis: 09 Polymers.pdf · pages 12, 13, 14, 15.

Part B · Q4 · 8 marks
(a) Explain the synthesis, properties, and applications of Kevlar polymer. [4 marks]
(b) What are biodegradable polymers? Give their important features and outline the applications of Polylactic Acid (PLA). [4 marks]+
PankusDesk-authored solution

(a) Explain the synthesis, properties, and applications of Kevlar polymer.

Kevlar is an aromatic polyamide (aramid), poly(p-phenylene terephthalamide). Monomers for the practical condensation route are p-phenylenediamine, H₂N–C₆H₄–NH₂, and terephthaloyl chloride, ClCO–C₆H₄–COCl. They form [–NH–C₆H₄–NH–CO–C₆H₄–CO–]ₙ with elimination of HCl (chain ends neglected in the repeating-unit scheme).

The supplied simplified scheme uses terephthalic acid instead of the acid chloride and eliminates water; it identifies the same aromatic amide backbone but should not be mistaken for the industrial reaction conditions. Rigid para-linked aromatic chains, alignment and intermolecular hydrogen bonding give high tensile strength relative to mass.

Properties: high tensile strength and stiffness relative to mass, low density, heat/flame resistance and low electrical conductivity. Applications: body armour and helmets, ropes/cables, tyre reinforcement, aerospace composites and protective clothing.

General chemistry is used here to distinguish the practical acid-chloride synthesis from the simplified acid-based scheme printed in the handout; both produce the same aromatic polyamide backbone.

Answer basis: 09 Polymers.pdf · pages 8, 9.

(b) What are biodegradable polymers? Give their important features and outline the applications of Polylactic Acid (PLA).

Biodegradable polymers break down through biological activity under suitable conditions. They can reduce persistent waste where appropriate collection/treatment is available, and allow temporary biomedical devices that gradually resorb. Biodegradable does not mean instant, harmless degradation in every environment.

PLA (polylactic acid) is an aliphatic polyester with repeating unit [–O–CH(CH₃)–CO–]ₙ. Ferment carbohydrate feedstocks to lactic acid, then form polymer by polycondensation (removing water) or convert to lactide and use ring-opening polymerisation. It is a thermoplastic; bio-based origin and biodegradability are separate classifications.

Features include renewable feedstock, transparency, useful stiffness and processing by extrusion/moulding/3D printing. Limitations include brittleness, heat resistance and slow breakdown in ordinary soil/marine environments. Industrial composting conditions are usually important. Uses: packaging/films/cups, fibres and 3D-printing filament, and suitable medical grades for sutures, drug delivery and tissue scaffolds.

Answer basis: 09 Polymers.pdf · pages 15, 16, 17.

Part B · Q5 · 8 marks
(a) What are nanomaterials? Explain the characterization of nanomaterials using Scanning Tunnelling Microscopy (STM). [4 marks]
(b) Explain how UV-Visible spectroscopy is useful in the analysis of dye pollutants in industrial effluents by taking Methylene blue as an example. [4 marks]+
PankusDesk-authored solution

(a) What are nanomaterials? Explain the characterization of nanomaterials using Scanning Tunnelling Microscopy (STM).

Nanomaterials have at least one relevant structural dimension approximately 1–100 nm. Their high surface-area/volume ratio and size-dependent structure can change optical, electrical, mechanical and catalytic properties compared with bulk material.

STM maps suitable electrically conducting surfaces using quantum tunnelling. Bring a sharp conducting tip (commonly tungsten or platinum–iridium) within a very small gap of the conducting sample and apply bias. A tunnelling current flows without ordinary mechanical contact. Sharpness, conductivity and mechanical stability make W/Pt–Ir useful; the tip material alone does not guarantee atomic resolution.

Components: tip, bias/current-measurement circuit, x–y–z piezoelectric scanner, coarse approach, feedback controller/computer and vibration isolation. At comparable bias/electronic conditions the current decreases approximately exponentially with gap: \(I\propto e^{-2\kappa s}\), not directly in proportion to distance. Electronic density of states also affects the signal.

Constant-current mode: feedback adjusts z to hold a selected current while scanning x–y; record z(x,y). Constant-height mode: hold nominal tip height, record current variations; use sufficiently flat samples to avoid collisions. Fixed height is not a fixed local gap on an uneven surface.

STM maps conducting nanomaterial surfaces at very high spatial resolution and can reveal steps, atomic arrangements, adsorbates and defects. It requires an electrically conducting path and is sensitive to vibration, tip condition and drift; it is not a universal method for all insulating nanomaterials.

Answer basis: 10 Spectroscopy and Smart Materials.pdf · pages 14, 15, 16; 1_Nano material.pdf · pages 5, 7.

(b) Explain how UV-Visible spectroscopy is useful in the analysis of dye pollutants in industrial effluents by taking Methylene blue as an example.

Principle: methylene blue absorbs visible light. Within a verified linear range, Beer–Lambert law gives \(A=\log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{I_0}{I}\right)=\varepsilon lc\), so absorbance at fixed path length is related to concentration.

A coloured dye such as methylene blue absorbs at a characteristic visible wavelength through electronic transitions. Use a solvent/reagent blank, scan a representative standard and select λmax (methylene blue is commonly measured near 664 nm in dilute water, but determine the actual maximum for the sample conditions).

Prepare several known standards covering the expected range. Measure their absorbances with the same cuvette/path length, blank-correct them and plot A against concentration. Fit A = mc + b over the verified linear range. Filter/clarify a turbid sample when appropriate without removing the analyte; dilute if needed. Measure the sample under identical conditions.

Calculate c_diluted = (A_sample − b)/m, then c_original = dilution factor × c_diluted. Report units, dilution and calibration range. For treatment studies, removal % = [(c_initial − c_final)/c_initial]×100, comparing consistent volume/basis. No numeric concentration can be obtained without measured absorbance and calibration/ε.

This supports monitoring dye effluents, treatment efficiency and discharge trends. Mixed dyes, pH changes and suspended particles may interfere; a single dye absorbance is not a universal measure of total organic pollution or COD. Source clarification: unsupported direct COD claims in the DOCX are not adopted.

Answer basis: 10 Spectroscopy and Smart Materials.pdf · pages 3, 4, 5.

Part B · Q6 · 8 marks
(a) What are self-healing materials? Explain the working principle of self-healing materials with suitable examples. [4 marks]
(b) Explain the working principle and applications of an amperometric glucose biosensor. [4 marks]+
PankusDesk-authored solution

(a) What are self-healing materials? Explain the working principle of self-healing materials with suitable examples.

Self-healing materials restore some lost integrity/function after damage through a built-in repair mechanism. Repair can be autonomic (damage triggers it) or externally activated (for example heat/light). It is not necessarily complete, unlimited or intervention-free.

Extrinsic capsule systems embed healing agent in microcapsules. A crack ruptures capsules, agent flows into the crack and reacts/polymerises, sometimes with an embedded catalyst. Vascular systems deliver agent through channels; replenishment can permit repeated repair. A depleted one-use capsule cannot heal indefinitely.

Intrinsic systems rely on reversible bonds/interactions or molecular mobility within the material, for example reversible Diels–Alder bonds or hydrogen-bond networks. Heating or another suitable stimulus allows damaged interfaces to reconnect; cooling/rebonding restores useful properties. Intrinsic/extrinsic and autonomic/non-autonomic describe different classification axes.

Applications: anticorrosion/scratch-resistant coatings, aircraft/vehicle composites, concrete systems, sealants, adhesives, pipelines and cables. Benefits include extended service life and lower maintenance; recovery depends on crack size, environment, agent supply and mechanical requirements. No source product lifetime is a universal guarantee.

Answer basis: 10 Spectroscopy and Smart Materials.pdf · pages 24, 25; unit_5_Notes.pdf · pages 8, 9.

(b) Explain the working principle and applications of an amperometric glucose biosensor.

An amperometric glucose biosensor uses immobilised glucose oxidase (GOx) as its biological recognition element and measures a reaction-related current at a controlled electrode potential. A working electrode, reference electrode and where required a counter electrode are connected to current-measuring electronics.

GOx catalyses glucose + O₂ → gluconolactone + H₂O₂; gluconolactone + H₂O → gluconic acid. At an oxidation-detection electrode: H₂O₂ → O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. The measured current is related by calibration to glucose over the usable range.

Overall acid-forming enzyme reaction: C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂ + H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₇ + H₂O₂. Water is included to balance the net reaction. Interfering oxidisable species, oxygen availability, enzyme ageing, temperature and fouling need control. Mediated and other commercial glucose sensors may use different electron-transfer schemes.

Applications include blood-glucose meters, appropriately designed continuous monitors, food/fermentation analysis and environmental sensing. Biosensing materials include immobilised GOx on a suitable conductive support; a support alone is not the biological recognition element. Benefits include selective, rapid and potentially portable analysis, subject to calibration and device-specific limitations.

Answer basis: 10 Spectroscopy and Smart Materials.pdf · pages 30.

Part A · (a) · 2 marksOutline the principle of methanol-oxygen fuel cell and mention the reactions at the respective electrodes.+

A direct methanol cell electrochemically oxidises supplied methanol and reduces oxygen. In an acidic/proton-conducting description: anode CH₃OH + H₂O → CO₂ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻; cathode 3/2 O₂ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 3H₂O. Overall CH₃OH + 3/2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Part A · (b) · 2 marksCorrosion of water filled steel tanks occurs below the water level. Give reasons.+
PankusDesk-authored solution

Differential-aeration corrosion can occur on one metal where oxygen access differs. The oxygen-poor region becomes anodic relative to the oxygen-rich region. Under a water drop, the centre is less aerated and iron dissolves there; the rim supports oxygen reduction. In a partly filled tank, attack is concentrated just below the waterline because that region is less oxygenated than the near-surface cathodic area.

Part A · (c) · 2 marksState Pilling-Bedworth rule and brief out its significance?+
PankusDesk-authored solution

The Pilling–Bedworth ratio is the volume of oxide formed divided by the volume of metal consumed. A ratio below 1 usually gives a discontinuous/non-protective film; a ratio roughly 1–2 often favours an adherent protective film; a much larger ratio can cause stress, cracking or spalling. It is an approximate guideline: adhesion, defects, volatility and environment also control protection.

Part A · (d) · 2 marksList out the differences of top-down and bottom-up approach of nanomaterials?+
PankusDesk-authored solution

Top-down synthesis reduces bulk material: mechanical/ball milling, lithography or suitable etching/ablation. Part B · Q1 · 4 marksWhat is knocking of an IC engine? Mention its ill effects & how can it be prevented?+
PankusDesk-authored solution

In a spark-ignition petrol engine, knocking is rapid autoignition of the unburnt end-gas ahead of the normal flame, causing pressure waves and a metallic sound. It lowers smoothness/efficiency and can damage parts. Suitable high-octane fuel, correct ignition timing, cooling and appropriate compression ratio help prevent it. Diesel knock arises from excessive ignition delay followed by rapid combustion; do not confuse the two mechanisms.

Ill effects include metallic noise and vibration, uneven/rapid pressure rise, loss of power and efficiency, overheating and damage to piston/cylinder components. In spark-ignition engines, use suitable high-octane fuel, correct ignition timing, avoid excessive compression ratio and maintain effective cooling and deposits control.

The question does not specify SI or CI. If discussing a compression-ignition diesel engine, state that diesel knock is associated with a long ignition delay and rapid burning of accumulated fuel. Suitable cetane quality, correct injection timing/atomisation and appropriate engine operating temperature reduce that delay; increasing petrol octane is not the remedy for diesel knock.

Original Unit 4 · Choose Q3 OR Q4

Part B · Q3 · 8 marks
(a) What is cathodic protection? Explain about impressed current cathodic protection. [4 marks]
(b) Passivity of a metal leads to more corrosion resistance. Why? [4 marks]+
PankusDesk-authored solution

(a) What is cathodic protection? Explain about impressed current cathodic protection.

Cathodic protection reduces metal dissolution by making the structure the cathode of an electrochemical circuit.

In impressed-current protection, connect the structure to the negative terminal of a DC source and a suitable auxiliary anode to its positive terminal. Current passes through the electrolyte from the auxiliary anode to the structure; the structure is polarised cathodically. It suits large pipelines, tanks and marine structures, with controllable output. It requires power, monitoring and care against overprotection/interference.

Electrons supplied by the negative terminal suppress oxidation of the protected steel; cathodic reduction (such as oxygen reduction in aerated water) is supported at its surface. The auxiliary anode supports the balancing oxidation. Correct polarity is essential: the structure connects to DC negative, not positive. Applications include long pipelines, storage tanks and marine structures.

Answer basis: Corrosion.pdf · pages 16, 17, 18.

(b) Passivity of a metal leads to more corrosion resistance. Why?

Passivity is a marked reduction of corrosion rate caused by a thin, adherent and relatively impervious surface film. The film restricts contact and transport of reacting species, reducing further metal oxidation even when the metal is thermodynamically active.

Examples: Al₂O₃ on aluminium and chromium-rich oxide on stainless steel. An intact/re-forming film gives high corrosion resistance; porous ordinary iron rust does not give equivalent protection. The film can fail locally in chloride solutions, at scratches or under unsuitable pH/potential, leading to pitting. Passivity therefore improves resistance under suitable conditions rather than guaranteeing immunity.

Part B · Q4 · 8 marksExplain the mechanism involved in the rusting of iron based on wet theory of corrosion.+
PankusDesk-authored solution

Wet corrosion requires anodic and cathodic regions connected through the metal and an electrolyte. Iron dissolves at anodic sites: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻. Electrons travel through the metal to cathodic sites; ionic conduction through the electrolyte completes the circuit.

Hydrogen-evolution mechanism (acidic, poorly aerated solution): cathodic reaction 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂↑. Combining it with iron oxidation gives Fe + 2H⁺ → Fe²⁺ + H₂. The iron loses metal while hydrogen bubbles evolve at cathodic sites. This acid-dissolution process does not by itself describe the formation of ordinary hydrated rust.

Oxygen-absorption mechanism (aerated neutral/alkaline water): cathodic reaction O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. Twice the iron anode reaction supplies four electrons. The resulting ions combine: Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂. Oxygen-poor regions tend to be anodic and oxygen-rich regions cathodic.

Part B · Q1 · 4 marksExplain the working of Lithium-ion battery.+
PankusDesk-authored solution

A lithium-ion cell shuttles Li⁺ reversibly between two intercalation electrodes. The supplied example uses graphite at the negative electrode, lithium cobalt oxide at the positive electrode, a separator and a non-aqueous lithium-salt electrolyte.

On discharge, the negative electrode releases lithium ions and electrons: LiC₆ → C₆ + Li⁺ + e⁻. The positive-electrode idealised reaction is CoO₂ + Li⁺ + e⁻ → LiCoO₂. Overall: LiC₆ + CoO₂ → C₆ + LiCoO₂. Li⁺ travels through the electrolyte and electrons through the external load toward the positive electrode.

Charging supplies external energy and reverses these transfers. The equations are idealised end-member bookkeeping; practical cells cycle over controlled partial states of charge, not complete conversion to pure CoO₂. Advantages include high energy density, relatively low self-discharge and long useful cycle life. Limitations include ageing, thermal-runaway risk and protection/charging requirements. Applications: phones, laptops, cameras, electric vehicles and storage.

Part B · Q4 · 8 marksWhat is differential aeration corrosion? Write a note on the following.
(a) Water line corrosion
(b) pitting corrosion+
PankusDesk-authored solution

The paper gives 8 marks for this question as a whole; no separate subpart marks are printed.

(a) Water line corrosion

Differential-aeration corrosion occurs when different parts of the same metal in an electrolyte receive unequal oxygen. The less-aerated region is anodic (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻); the more-aerated region is cathodic (O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻).

In a partly water-filled steel tank, the near-surface oxygen-rich region acts cathodically relative to the less-aerated region just below it. Dissolution is concentrated below the waterline; ferrous ions and hydroxide subsequently form hydroxides/rust. The waterline zone is thus vulnerable to a concentration-cell attack.

(b) pitting corrosion

Pitting begins at local passive-film breakdown/occluded regions. A small active pit is coupled to a large surrounding cathodic surface, concentrating anodic current. Restricted transport and chloride-rich acidic conditions can sustain the pit. Chloride solution also increases electrolyte conductivity and can disrupt passive films; not every salt affects every metal equally.

The small pit is the anode, Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻; the large surrounding oxygenated surface acts as cathode. Metal-ion hydrolysis can acidify the pit, while restricted exchange and chloride concentration sustain attack. A deep pit can perforate metal despite small overall mass loss.

Part B · Q4 · 8 marks
(a) Differentiate between galvanizing and tinning. [4 marks]

PankusDesk-authored solution

(a) Differentiate between galvanizing and tinning.

Galvanising coats iron/steel with zinc. Intact zinc is a barrier; if scratched in a suitable electrolyte, zinc remains more active and dissolves sacrificially, protecting exposed iron. Tinning coats with tin, a more noble metal: intact tin is a barrier, but a scratch can create an iron-anode/tin-cathode couple and accelerate local steel attack. Thus galvanising offers sacrificial protection after small coating damage, unlike ordinary tinning.

Galvanising: zinc coating, more active than iron; barrier plus sacrificial protection, including at a small scratch. Typical use: roofing, pipes and outdoor steel. Tinning: tin coating, more noble than iron; barrier protection while intact, but exposed iron at a scratch becomes anodic and may corrode rapidly. Typical use: appropriately protected food containers/utensils.

Answer basis: Unit-2 Corrosion (1).pdf · pages 13, 14.

(b) Define cathodic protection. Explain sacrificial anode protection.

Cathodic protection suppresses metal dissolution by making the structure the cathode. In the sacrificial-anode method, connect a more active metal such as Mg, Zn or a suitable Al alloy to the steel in the electrolyte. The sacrificial metal oxidises (for example Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻) and supplies electrons to the steel. Inspect and replace consumed anodes. No external DC supply is needed, but available driving voltage/current is limited.

Connect the steel structure electrically to a suitably sized sacrificial anode in the same electrolyte. The anode oxidation supplies electrons; reduction takes place on the protected steel instead of its dissolution. For example Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. Uses include buried pipelines, water heaters and ship hulls. Ensure electrical continuity and inspect/replace the consumed anodes; the method needs no external DC source.

(b) Discuss the methods of synthesis of nanomaterials. [4 marks]+
PankusDesk-authored solution

(a) Compare the working principle, advantages and disadvantages involved in STM and AFM.

Principle: STM detects quantum tunnelling current between a biased sharp conducting tip and a conducting sample; AFM detects tip–surface forces through cantilever deflection/oscillation. Both scan with fine piezoelectric positioning and use feedback.

STM modes: constant current (feedback changes height) and constant height (record current variations). AFM modes: contact, non-contact and tapping. STM requires a conducting path; AFM can examine insulating as well as conducting surfaces.

STM advantages: atomic-scale surface/electronic-state information and atom manipulation under suitable conditions. Disadvantages: conducting samples required, sensitive to vibration/tip contamination, small scan area and specialised operation.

AFM advantages: wide sample range, including polymers and biological samples, operation in air/liquid with suitable equipment, and topography plus mechanical-property mapping. Disadvantages: tip-shape convolution, possible sample damage, drift and relatively slow/small-area scans. Neither instrument is universally faster or higher-resolution for every sample.

(b) Discuss the methods of synthesis of nanomaterials.

Top-down synthesis reduces bulk material: mechanical/ball milling, lithography or suitable etching/ablation. It can suit patterned manufacture but may introduce defects, contamination and size-distribution limits. Bottom-up synthesis builds from atoms/molecules: precipitation, sol–gel, chemical vapour deposition or self-assembly. It permits compositional/size control under suitable conditions but requires control of nucleation, growth and aggregation.

Top-down example—ball milling: place coarse solid and hard balls in a mill; repeated impact/fracture and deformation reduce particle size. Control milling conditions/time, contamination and agglomeration, then separate/classify the product.

Bottom-up example—sol–gel: hydrolyse a suitable molecular precursor, condense it to form a colloidal sol/network, allow gelation, then dry and heat/treat as required to obtain the desired oxide nanomaterial. Nucleation/growth and aggregation must be controlled. Top-down and bottom-up are the two approaches, not universal recipes for all materials.

Part B · Q6 · 8 marksExplain in detail the following mechanisms of lubrication
(a) Thick film lubrication
(b) Boundary lubrication/thin film lubrication+
PankusDesk-authored solution

The paper gives 8 marks for this question as a whole; no separate subpart marks are printed.

(a) Thick film lubrication

Hydrodynamic/thick-film lubrication: a continuous viscous oil film separates surfaces; motion and geometry develop load-carrying pressure. Low friction results mainly from shearing fluid, not metal contact. It suits adequate speed and appropriate load/viscosity; a suitable mineral or synthetic oil can be used. The supplied course examples classify watches, clocks and sewing machines under fluid-film lubrication; use that stated course classification while recognising that a real instrument’s lubrication regime depends on its load, speed and geometry.

A continuous oil film thicker than the surface roughness prevents metal-to-metal contact. Relative motion entrains oil into a converging gap; hydrodynamic pressure supports the load. Friction arises chiefly from shear within the fluid. A suitable viscosity, adequate oil supply, speed and geometry are needed; loss of the film at startup or high load can change the regime.

(b) Boundary lubrication/thin film lubrication

Boundary/thin-film lubrication: at low speed, high load or startup, a full hydrodynamic film may not form. Polar molecules/antiwear additives adsorb or react on surfaces to provide a protective thin layer. Fatty oils/blends or formulated oils can provide boundary lubricity. Heavy loads and slow speeds commonly require this mechanism; delicate low-load instruments may use low-viscosity oils, with the actual regime determined by speed/load/clearance.

Polar lubricant molecules attach to the metal surface with their long hydrocarbon portions directed outward. The resulting adherent thin layer reduces direct metallic contact and shear/wear where full-film separation cannot be sustained. It is useful for starting/stopping, low-speed heavily loaded contacts and suitable formulated gear oils; films can fail under extreme pressure/temperature.
