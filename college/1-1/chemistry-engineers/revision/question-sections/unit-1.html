Assumption: the phrase “dissolved salts” is taken literally, so the listed CaCO₃ contributes 100 mg/L of dissolved carbonate hardness. Assume no removal of Ca/Mg by precipitation. If CaCO₃ instead means undissolved solid, the question needs a different dissolution/equilibrium model; the answer below is for the stated dissolved-salt interpretation.

\(\mathrm{CaCO_3}:100\times\dfrac{100}{100}=100\) mg/L as CaCO₃.

\(\mathrm{Mg(HCO_3)_2}:14.6\times\dfrac{100}{146}=10\) mg/L as CaCO₃ — not the table’s 100.

\(\mathrm{MgSO_4}:12\times\dfrac{100}{120}=10\) mg/L as CaCO₃.

\(\mathrm{CaCl_2}:11.1\times\dfrac{100}{111}=10\) mg/L as CaCO₃.

NaCl has no Ca/Mg hardness contribution. HCl does not add calcium or magnesium, so it must not be added to total hardness.

Total hardness = 100 + 10 + 10 + 10 = 130 ppm as CaCO₃. Before accounting for acid, carbonate hardness is 100 + 10 = 110 ppm and non-carbonate hardness is 10 + 10 = 20 ppm.

HCl consumes carbonate/bicarbonate alkalinity, converting an equivalent amount of carbonate (temporary) hardness into non-carbonate (permanent) hardness. It changes the classification, while the total Ca/Mg hardness remains 130 ppm under the assumption above.

A representative conversion is \(\mathrm{CaCO_3+2HCl\rightarrow CaCl_2+H_2O+CO_2}\). Thus 2 mol HCl neutralize alkalinity equivalent to 1 mol CaCO₃.

Use M(HCl) = 36.5 g/mol (H = 1, Cl = 35.5) and M(CaCO₃) = 100 g/mol. The printed concentration remains 36 mg/L; it is not changed to 36.5 mg/L.

\[A=36\times\dfrac{100}{2\times36.5}=36\times\dfrac{50}{36.5}=49.315068\ldots\text{ mg/L as CaCO}_3\]

Acid is insufficient to consume all 110 ppm of carbonate hardness. Temporary hardness = 110 − 49.315068… = 60.684931… ppm. Permanent hardness = 20 + 49.315068… = 69.315068… ppm.

Check: 60.684931… + 69.315068… = 130 ppm. The acidity is subtracted from temporary hardness and added to permanent hardness exactly once.

Using the source chart’s rounded 1 ppm = 0.1 °Fr = 0.07 °Cl: temporary hardness = 6.068493… °Fr ≈ 4.247945… °Cl; permanent hardness = 6.931506… °Fr ≈ 4.852054… °Cl. Total hardness = 13 °Fr ≈ 9.10 °Cl.

Final answer: Using the printed question and stated assumption: temporary hardness ≈ 60.68 ppm = 6.068 °Fr ≈ 4.248 °Cl; permanent hardness ≈ 69.32 ppm = 6.932 °Fr ≈ 4.852 °Cl; total hardness = 130 ppm = 13 °Fr ≈ 9.10 °Cl.

Short answer 1 Name the salts that cause temporary hardness. How are they removed? [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Temporary hardness is mainly caused by calcium bicarbonate, Ca(HCO₃)₂, and magnesium bicarbonate, Mg(HCO₃)₂. Boiling removes it by decomposing the bicarbonates and separating insoluble CaCO₃ or Mg(OH)₂.

\(\mathrm{Ca(HCO_3)_2\xrightarrow{heat}CaCO_3\downarrow+H_2O+CO_2\uparrow}\)
\(\mathrm{Mg(HCO_3)_2\xrightarrow{heat}Mg(OH)_2\downarrow+2CO_2\uparrow}\)
Short answer 2 Mention the different units of hardness? Give the relation between the units. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

The principal units are mg/L as CaCO₃, ppm as CaCO₃, degrees French (°Fr) and degrees Clarke (°Cl). For dilute water, 1 mg/L ≈ 1 ppm.

\(1\text{ ppm}=0.1\,^{\circ}\mathrm{Fr}\approx0.07\,^{\circ}\mathrm{Cl}\)
\(1\,^{\circ}\mathrm{Fr}=10\text{ ppm};\quad1\,^{\circ}\mathrm{Cl}\approx14.3\text{ ppm}\)
The source gives rounded conversions. Numerical answers here consistently use 0.07 °Cl per ppm rather than mixing that factor with the separately rounded reciprocal 14.3.
Short answer 3 List out the disadvantages of usage of hard water in industries. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer
  • Boilers: scale/sludge formation, poor heat transfer, fuel wastage, overheating and corrosion-related troubles.
  • Textile/dyeing and laundry: soap wastage, scum, stains and uneven colour.
  • Paper: poor finish and staining from iron salts.
  • Sugar: interference with refining/crystallisation.
  • Concrete and pharmaceutical manufacture: unwanted salts can interfere with product quality.
Short answer 4 Hardness is expressed in terms of CaCO3 equivalents? Give reason. [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Different hardness-producing substances have different molecular masses. Expressing each contribution as an equivalent amount of CaCO₃ puts them on one common chemical-equivalence basis so that contributions can be added and compared. CaCO₃ has a convenient molecular mass of 100 and equivalent mass of 50.

\(\text{CaCO}_3\text{ equivalent}=\text{concentration}\times\dfrac{50}{\text{equivalent mass of substance}}\)
Short answer 5 Mention the dissolved salts that cause temporary and permanent hardness in water. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer
  • Temporary: Ca(HCO₃)₂ and Mg(HCO₃)₂.
  • Permanent: CaCl₂, MgCl₂, CaSO₄, MgSO₄, Ca(NO₃)₂ and Mg(NO₃)₂. Permanent hardness is not removed by simple boiling.
Short answer 6 Give the principle involved in the estimation of hardness by EDTA method. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

At about pH 10, calcium and magnesium ions form stable complexes with EDTA. Eriochrome Black T (EBT) first gives a wine-red metal–indicator complex. EDTA binds the metal ions more strongly, releasing the blue indicator at the endpoint. The standard EDTA volume measures the hardness.

Short answer 7 Why is ammonical (NH4Cl+NH4OH) buffer added during the determination of hardness in water samples? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

NH₄Cl/NH₄OH buffer maintains pH close to 10, the conditions needed for the EDTA complexation and a clear EBT endpoint. If the pH drifts, metal–EDTA/indicator equilibria change and the endpoint becomes unreliable.

Short answer 10 What is internal water treatment in boilers and why is it necessary? [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Internal treatment means adding suitable chemicals directly to boiler water. They either convert scale-forming impurities into loose sludge removable by blow-down or keep hardness ions in soluble complexes. It reduces adherent deposits, heat-transfer loss and overheating. Examples are Calgon, phosphate and colloidal conditioning.

Short answer 12 What is disinfection? What are the different methods of disinfection? [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Disinfection is the destruction of disease-producing microorganisms in water. The supplied course material discusses boiling and chemical disinfection by chlorine, bleaching powder and chloramine. Chlorination produces hypochlorous acid (HOCl), responsible for much of the germicidal action.

Short answer 13 Why is chloramine preferred to bleaching powder during disinfection? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Chloramine provides a longer-lasting disinfectant residual because it is more persistent than free chlorine. In the supplied comparison it also avoids the objectionable taste/odour associated with excess bleaching powder and does not introduce the calcium component of bleaching powder. Its advantage here is persistent residual protection.

Short answer 14 How does break point chlorination improve water quality in municipal water supplies? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Chlorine is first consumed by reducing substances and ammonia/organic matter. Sufficient further addition destroys much of the combined chlorine. Beyond the breakpoint, a controllable free-chlorine residual can remain for disinfection rather than all added chlorine being consumed by the water’s demand.

Short answer 15 What happens if natural water is fed into boilers? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Evaporation concentrates dissolved salts. Untreated feed water can cause scale and sludge, priming/foaming (carryover), boiler corrosion and caustic embrittlement. Deposits lower heat transfer, waste fuel, choke passages and may cause overheating or failure.

Short answer 20 In demineralization process, water is usually passed through cation exchanger and then through anion exchanger. Give reason. [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

The cation bed removes Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ and releases H⁺, producing acidic water. The following anion bed replaces anions with OH⁻, which combines with H⁺ to form water. Removing hardness cations first also avoids exposing them to the alkaline conditions an anion-first sequence could create, with precipitates fouling the bed.

\(\mathrm{H^+ +OH^-\rightarrow H_2O}\)

PankusDesk answer

Calgon is sodium hexametaphosphate, (NaPO₃)₆, also written Na₂[Na₄(PO₃)₆]. It complexes calcium into a soluble species, preventing calcium-salt scale. The supplied notation gives:

Short answer 22 Why is calgon conditioning much preferred over phosphate conditioning? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Calgon keeps calcium in a soluble complex. Phosphate conditioning instead precipitates calcium/magnesium phosphates as soft sludge, which must be removed by blow-down. Thus Calgon’s advantage in this comparison is suppression of precipitated deposits/sludge rather than merely changing a hard deposit into a loose one. Treatment choice still depends on boiler conditions.

Short answer 23 State and explain the principle involved in reverse osmosis process? [BL-1] +
PankusDesk answer

A pressure greater than the osmotic pressure is applied to the concentrated/brackish-water side of a semipermeable membrane. Water then moves from the concentrated side to the dilute/pure-water side, opposite to spontaneous osmosis, while most dissolved salts are retained.

Short answer 24 List any two advantages of nanofiltration. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer
  • It removes many divalent ions and is useful for water softening.
  • It rejects many small organic pollutants while generally requiring lower pressure than reverse osmosis.

Essay 1 Explain in detail how hardness of a water sample is estimated by EDTA method. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Principle: disodium EDTA complexes Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺. At pH about 10, EBT forms a wine-red metal–indicator complex; EDTA displaces the indicator, giving a blue endpoint.

Prepare standard hard water containing 1 g CaCO₃ equivalent per litre (1 mg/mL): dissolve pure CaCO₃ in minimum dilute HCl, evaporate to dryness, redissolve and make to 1 L.

Standardise EDTA: pipette 50 mL standard hard water, add 10–15 mL buffer and 4–5 drops EBT. Titrate to clear blue. Let the titre be V₁ mL. The aliquot contains 50 mg CaCO₃ equivalent.

Total hardness: titrate 50 mL of the water sample in the same way; let the titre be V₂ mL.

Permanent hardness: boil 250 mL of sample down to about 50 mL, filter off the precipitate and wash it. Collect filtrate and washings and make back to 250 mL. Titrate a 50 mL aliquot; let the titre be V₃ mL. Restoring the original volume avoids an artificial concentration change.

Temporary hardness is total minus permanent hardness. The equations below follow the 50 mL aliquots and standard-water concentration specified above.

\(\mathrm{[M-EBT]+EDTA\rightarrow[M-EDTA]+EBT}\quad\text{(wine red → blue)}\)
\(1\text{ mL EDTA}=\dfrac{50}{V_1}\text{ mg CaCO}_3\text{ equivalent}\)
\(H_{total}=\dfrac{V_2(50/V_1)\times1000}{50}=\dfrac{1000V_2}{V_1}\text{ ppm}\)
\(H_{permanent}=\dfrac{1000V_3}{V_1}\text{ ppm}\)
\(H_{temporary}=\dfrac{1000(V_2-V_3)}{V_1}\text{ ppm}\)

Essay 4 Explain the stages involved in the process of break-point chlorination with necessary illustration. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Breakpoint chlorination is the addition of enough chlorine to satisfy the water’s chlorine demand and pass through the destruction of combined chlorine, after which increasing free residual chlorine can be obtained. Read the supplied graph with chlorine dose on the horizontal axis and chlorine residual on the vertical axis.

  • Up to A: reducing substances consume chlorine; little or no residual is measured.
  • A–B: reaction with ammonia/nitrogen-containing substances forms chloramines; combined residual rises.
  • B–C: further chlorine destroys chloramines and other combined-chlorine compounds, so residual falls.
  • At C: the breakpoint is reached. Much of the chlorine demand/combined chlorine has been dealt with.
  • Beyond C: additional chlorine gives an increasing free-chlorine residual, providing disinfection after an appropriate contact period.
The source transcript’s wording is preserved above. The breakpoint does not mean all chlorine addition must cease in every treatment process; the purpose beyond it is an appropriate disinfectant residual.
Essay 5 Define scales and sludges? How are they formed? Give their disadvantages and removal methods. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

As boiler water evaporates, dissolved salts concentrate and precipitate. Sludge is a soft, loose, slimy deposit, often accumulating in cooler/slow-flow parts. Scale is a hard adherent coating on the boiler wall.

  • CaCO₃ scale: heating decomposes calcium bicarbonate.
  • CaSO₄ scale: solubility decreases at high boiler temperatures.
  • Mg(OH)₂ deposits: hydrolysis/decomposition of magnesium salts.
  • Silica/silicate deposits: calcium/magnesium silicates stick firmly to the wall.
  • Consequences: poor heat transfer and fuel wastage, choked pipes/valves, lower efficiency, overheating, weakened tubes and possible failure or explosion.
  • Sludge removal: blow-down and brushing; reduce sludge formation by suitable feed-water softening.
  • Scale removal: scraper/wire brushing for loose deposits, suitable chemical treatment for the deposit and controlled thermal treatment where appropriate. The notes specify dilute HCl for carbonate scale and EDTA treatment for calcium/magnesium scales.
  • Prevention: external feed-water treatment plus internal conditioning and removal of accumulated sludge.

Essay 6 Discuss the reasons and control methods for the boiler trouble caused by scale formation. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Scale forms as salts concentrate and become insoluble under boiler conditions. Important causes are bicarbonate decomposition, reduced CaSO₄ solubility at high temperature, magnesium-salt hydrolysis and silica/silicate deposition.

  • External control: remove Ca/Mg salts before the feed water enters the boiler, for example by ion exchange.
  • Calgon conditioning: keep calcium in soluble complexes.
  • Phosphate conditioning: convert hardness salts into soft, non-adherent phosphate sludge and remove it by blow-down.
  • Colloidal conditioning: organic substances coat precipitates and help form loose, non-sticky deposits, particularly in low-pressure treatment described by the notes.
  • Remove existing deposits using a method suited to the deposit and equipment; avoid treating every scale as an acid-soluble carbonate.

Essay 8 Discuss various steps involved in the treatment of potable water. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer
  • Screening: retain floating debris such as leaves, wood and large particles on screens.
  • Sedimentation: allow suspended matter to settle under gravity.
  • Coagulation/flocculation: add a suitable coagulant, mix, and form gelatinous flocs which capture fine suspended/colloidal impurities; allow these flocs to settle. Alum can produce Al(OH)₃ floc in water with suitable alkalinity.

Essay 9 Explain the purification of water by Ion exchange process. Give the reactions involved. [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Ion-exchange resins are insoluble cross-linked polymers with exchangeable ions. A cation exchanger in the H⁺ form removes calcium, magnesium and other cations. An anion exchanger in the OH⁻ form removes anions. The released H⁺ and OH⁻ combine to form water.

Cation bed: hard water first passes through acidic resin, represented RH. Two H⁺ sites are needed to replace one divalent ion.

Anion bed: the acidic effluent passes through basic resin, represented R′OH. Anions are exchanged for hydroxyl ions.

Regenerate exhausted cation resin with dilute mineral acid and anion resin with dilute NaOH; rinse the beds before reuse. Turbid feed must be clarified to avoid resin-bed fouling.

Essay 12 What is desalination? Explain the process of desalination of water by reverse osmosis. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Desalination is removal of dissolved salts from saline or brackish water to obtain lower-salinity water. In normal osmosis, water moves through a semipermeable membrane from the dilute side towards the more concentrated side.

In reverse osmosis, pressure greater than the osmotic pressure is applied to the concentrated side. Water is forced across the membrane towards the dilute/pure-water side; most dissolved salts are retained in the concentrated reject stream.

A simple cell has a pressurised saline-water compartment, a semipermeable membrane and a permeate outlet. The supplied diagram shows the pressure applied through a piston, with pure water leaving below the membrane.

Advantages include compact equipment, continuous operation and no evaporation/condensation phase change. Limitations include pressure/energy requirements, membrane fouling and the need to manage the concentrate.
