Short answer 2 · Electrode potential and standard potential What is electrode potential? Define standard electrode potential. [BL-1] +
PankusDesk answer

Electrode potential is the potential developed when an electrode is in contact with a solution of its ions; it expresses its tendency to undergo oxidation or reduction. Standard electrode potential, E°, is measured relative to SHE under specified standard conditions. The notes state 25°C, 1 bar gas pressure and 1 M ion concentration. SHE is assigned 0 V.

Short answer 3 · Nernst equation and significance Write down Nernst equation and outline its significance? [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

For the stated reaction, \(E=E^\circ-\dfrac{RT}{nF}\ln Q\). At 298 K, \(E=E^\circ-\dfrac{0.0591}{n}\log_{10}Q\) V. Q is the activity-based reaction quotient; R is the gas constant, T temperature, n the electron number and F the Faraday constant.

The equation determines how potential changes with composition and temperature and supports cell-voltage and concentration-cell calculations. At equilibrium Q = K and the full-cell EMF is zero. R25 requires no derivation.

Short answer 5 · Why a stable reference is needed Why do we need a stable reference electrode in electrochemical cells? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

An isolated half-cell potential cannot be measured directly without a second electrode. A reference electrode supplies a known, stable potential. Measuring the cell EMF relative to it allows the unknown electrode potential to be calculated, with reproducible results.

Short answer 6 · Ideal reference-electrode characteristics What characteristics make an ideal reference electrode? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

An ideal reference electrode has a stable, well-defined and reproducible potential; is non-polarizable under small measurement currents; is chemically compatible with the test solution; and is easy to prepare, maintain and handle. Its electrolyte composition and temperature should remain controlled.

Short answer 7 · SHE working principle State the working principle of the Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE). [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Platinum black catalyses the reversible equilibrium \(2\mathrm H^++2\mathrm e^-\rightleftharpoons\mathrm H_2\). Hydrogen is passed over the platinum surface in contact with hydrogen ions at the specified standard conditions. SHE can act as an anode (hydrogen oxidation) or a cathode (hydrogen-ion reduction); its standard reference potential is defined as zero.

Short answer 8 · Why SHE is primary Why is SHE called a primary reference electrode? [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

SHE is the primary reference because the standard hydrogen electrode is assigned E° = 0 V by convention. Other standard electrode potentials are determined relative to that reference, establishing the hydrogen scale. Secondary references such as calomel are calibrated against it.

Short answer 9 · Calomel advantage and limitation Mention one advantage and one limitation of the Calomel electrode. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Advantage: it provides a stable, reproducible potential and is easier to set up and maintain than a hydrogen-gas electrode. Limitation: it contains toxic mercury; its potential also depends on temperature and KCl concentration.

Short answer 10 · Function of an ion-selective electrode What is the function of an ion-selective electrode? [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

An ISE develops a membrane potential that responds preferentially to the activity of a selected ion in a sample. Measured against a reference electrode and calibrated with standards, that potential is used to estimate the target-ion activity/concentration. The glass electrode is selective for H⁺ and is used for pH measurement.

Short answer 16 · Pilling–Bedworth rule What is Pilling-Bedworth rule? Give its significance. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Pilling–Bedworth ratio is the volume of oxide formed divided by the volume of metal consumed. An oxide with insufficient volume cannot continuously cover the metal; an adherent non-porous film can reduce oxygen access.

The simplified course rule is useful for the supplied exercise, but real protection also depends on film integrity, stress, transport and environment. Large ratios can produce buckling, so “larger always means less corrosion” is not a universal law.

Short answer 18 · Corrosion beneath a tank waterline Corrosion of water filled steel tanks occurs below the water level. Give reasons. [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Oxygen concentration is lower just below the waterline than at the well-aerated surface. A differential-aeration cell forms: the less-aerated region is anodic and dissolves, while the more-aerated region is cathodic. Attack is therefore concentrated just beneath the water meniscus.

Short answer 20 · Corrosion beneath a water drop The metal under the water drop undergoes accelerated corrosion. Why? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

The edge of a water drop receives oxygen more readily than its centre. The oxygen-poor central region becomes anodic and dissolves; the oxygen-rich edge supports the cathodic oxygen-reduction reaction. This localized differential-aeration cell accelerates attack beneath the drop.

Short answer 21 · Why matching metals for nuts and bolts Nuts and bolts should be made of the same metal. Why? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Dissimilar metals in electrical contact and exposed to moisture can form a galvanic couple. The more active member becomes the anode and corrodes. Using the same metal avoids the dissimilar-metal potential difference and reduces this cause of corrosion.

Short answer 22 · Why small anodic areas corrode intensely Small anodic areas result in intense corrosion. Give the reason. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

A large cathode can demand a substantial total anodic current. If dissolution is concentrated on a small anode, the anodic current density (current per unit area) becomes high. Metal loss per unit anodic area therefore increases; a small steel pipe connected to a large copper tank is the supplied example.

Short answer 23 · Passivity and corrosion resistance Passivity results in more corrosion resistance. Why? [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Passivity is unusually high corrosion resistance caused by a thin, adherent protective surface film. The film separates the metal from the environment and slows further reaction. The notes describe passive films as insoluble, non-porous and capable of reforming under suitable oxidizing conditions.

Short answer 24 · Steel pipe connected to copper Why does corrosion occur in steel pipe connected to copper plumbing? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Steel and copper in contact through plumbing water form a galvanic couple. Iron is the more active metal and becomes the anode, while copper acts as cathode. Iron dissolves as Fe²⁺. Attack is especially severe if a small steel area is connected to a much larger copper area.

Short answer 25 · Iron versus aluminium corrosion Iron corrodes faster than Al, even though iron is placed below Al in the galvanic series. Why? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Position in a galvanic series alone does not determine the observed rate. Aluminium develops an adherent protective oxide film and becomes passive. Ordinary rust on iron does not provide comparable durable protection, so iron can corrode faster even though aluminium is more active.

Short answer 26 · Effect of temperature Why does the rate of metallic corrosion increase with the increase in temperature. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

The supplied notes explain that increasing temperature increases reaction rates and diffusion rates, generally accelerating corrosion under the stated conditions. The actual rate still depends on the metal, electrolyte and protective film.

Short answer 27 · Impurities and corrosion Impure metal corrodes faster than pure metal under identical conditions. Give the reason. [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Impurities create heterogeneity and local electrochemical cells. Anodic portions of the metal dissolve while cathodic impurity regions support reduction. The resulting local galvanic action can make an impure metal corrode faster than a pure specimen under otherwise identical conditions.

Short answer 28 · Iron beneath salt-solution drops Iron corrodes under drops of salt solution. Give the reason. [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

A salt-solution drop supplies a conducting electrolyte and unequal oxygen access. The less-aerated region under the drop is anodic; the better-aerated region is cathodic. Iron dissolves at the anode and oxygen reduction occurs at the cathode, producing localized corrosion.

Short answer 29 · Impure versus pure zinc A piece of impure zinc and pure zinc are placed in a salt solution. Which will metal corrode faster? Give the reason. [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Impure zinc corrodes faster under the stated identical conditions. Impurities such as Fe or Pb form local cathodic sites with zinc as the anodic metal; zinc dissolution is accelerated by these small electrochemical cells. Pure zinc has fewer such heterogeneities.

Final answer: Impure zinc corrodes faster under the stated conditions.

Short answer 32 · Cathodic protection and galvanization How is cathodic protection of iron different from its galvanization. [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Cathodic protection describes the electrochemical method of making iron the cathode, either by an attached sacrificial anode or by impressed current. Galvanization is the application of a zinc coating to iron: the intact coating acts as a barrier, and zinc can additionally provide sacrificial protection where iron is exposed. Thus galvanization is a coating process with a sacrificial action, while cathodic protection is the broader electrochemical protection principle.

The supplied bank asks about galvanization, but the notes do not develop it as a separate coating lesson. This is a PankusDesk explanation connecting the bank wording to the supplied zinc–iron sacrificial mechanism.

Short answer 33 · Cracked zinc coating Zinc-coated iron article is protected from rusting, even when the coating is cracked. Explain. [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

When a crack exposes iron to moisture, zinc and iron remain electrically connected. Zinc is more active and dissolves as the sacrificial anode; iron is the cathode and is protected while sufficient connected zinc remains. This extends protection beyond the coating’s physical barrier.

Short answer 34 · Why zinc is sacrificial Why is coating of zinc on iron called sacrificial anode? [BL-3] +
PankusDesk answer

Zinc is more active than iron in the supplied galvanic example. In contact with iron and an electrolyte, zinc oxidizes preferentially and supplies electrons, making iron cathodic. Zinc is consumed to protect the iron, hence the term sacrificial anode.

Essay 1 · Construction and working of a galvanic cell Explain the construction and working of an electrochemical (galvanic) cell with a neat, labelled diagram. +
PankusDesk answer

A galvanic cell converts the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy. In the Daniell cell, a zinc electrode is immersed in ZnSO₄ solution and a copper electrode in CuSO₄ solution. A salt bridge containing an inert electrolyte joins the half-cells, and an external wire with a voltmeter/load joins the metal electrodes.

The cell notation is \(\mathrm{Zn}|\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}(1\,\mathrm M)||\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(1\,\mathrm M)|\mathrm{Cu}\). Zinc is the negative anode and copper the positive cathode.

At the anode: \(\mathrm{Zn}\rightarrow\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}+2\mathrm e^-\). At the cathode: \(\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}+2\mathrm e^-\rightarrow\mathrm{Cu}\). Adding the half-reactions gives \(\mathrm{Zn}+\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}\rightarrow\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}+\mathrm{Cu}\).

Electrons pass through the external wire from zinc to copper. The salt bridge carries ionic current and maintains charge balance: anions move towards the zinc half-cell as Zn²⁺ forms, and cations towards the copper half-cell as Cu²⁺ is consumed. Oxidation and reduction remain spatially separated but electrically coupled.

The labelled Daniell-cell diagram below shows the separated half-cells, salt bridge and electron path.

Final answer: Chemical → electrical energy; zinc oxidizes, copper ions reduce, and electrons flow externally from Zn to Cu.

Essay 3 · Combined glass electrode Explain the construction, working, and applications of a Combined Glass Electrode as an ion-selective electrode./ Describe how a Combined Glass Electrode functions as an Ion-Selective Electrode (ISE). +
PankusDesk answer

A combined glass electrode is an H⁺-selective pH sensor that contains the glass measuring system and a reference system in one convenient assembly.

Construction: the thin glass bulb is made from pH-sensitive glass. The supplied notes specify 72% SiO₂, 22% Na₂O and 6% CaO. An internal Ag/AgCl electrode contacts a fixed internal solution (0.1 N HCl in the notes). A second reference compartment contains KCl and contacts the sample through a porous junction. The original diagram labels both reference systems and the thin membrane.

Working: hydration of the glass surface produces an ion-exchange layer. H⁺ interaction at the inner and outer surfaces gives a membrane potential related to the difference in hydrogen-ion activity. The internal solution is held fixed, so changes in sample pH change the measured voltage relative to the reference.

Calibrate with buffer solutions, immerse the membrane and reference junction in the sample, and measure with a high-impedance pH meter. Calibration establishes the practical voltage offset and response. Do not arbitrarily set the combined probe’s offset to zero.

Advantages: compact single assembly, convenient routine use and H⁺ selectivity; it can be used with coloured/turbid samples. Limitations: fragile membrane, calibration and temperature dependence, ageing and interfering-ion response; the source specifically warns about alkaline error above pH 12.

Applications include laboratory pH, industrial process control, environmental monitoring, biological fluids and food/beverage analysis. The original combined-probe diagram and glass-membrane illustration are included below.

With EG = EG° + 0.0592 pH in the existing book convention, E(SCE) − EG has slope −0.0592 V per pH at 25°C. The offset must be calibrated.

Essay 4 · Finding an unknown electrode potential Analyse how different reference electrodes help in determining the electrode potential of unknown electrodes. +
PankusDesk answer

A single half-cell potential is measured relative to a second, known electrode. Connect the unknown half-cell and reference half-cell through a suitable ionic junction and connect the electrical leads to measure the cell EMF with negligible current.

Keep the cell orientation and reduction-potential convention explicit: \(E_{\mathrm{cell}}=E_{\mathrm{right}}-E_{\mathrm{left}}\). If the reference is on the left, \(E_{\mathrm{unknown}}=E_{\mathrm{cell}}+E_{\mathrm{reference}}\); if it is on the right, \(E_{\mathrm{unknown}}=E_{\mathrm{reference}}-E_{\mathrm{cell}}\).

With SHE, use the specified reference conditions and E° = 0 V for standard-potential determination. Its reproducibility defines the potential scale, but gas purity, platinum cleanliness and control of pressure/activity make routine use demanding.

With calomel, use its known potential for the actual KCl concentration and temperature. It is convenient for routine measurements, but use the correct concentration-dependent reference value and compatible sample chemistry.

An unknown metal electrode is a working electrode; the glass membrane is instead an ion-selective measuring element for pH. In a combined probe it is measured against its incorporated reference. A stable reference allows changes in the measured voltage to be attributed to the working/sensing electrode rather than reference drift.

Final answer: Calculate the unknown potential from the measured cell EMF, the known reference potential and the explicitly recorded cell orientation.
Essay 5 · Wet corrosion and rusting Explain the mechanism involved in the rusting of iron by the wet theory of corrosion./ Discuss in detail the mechanism of electrochemical corrosion theory with the evolution of hydrogen and absorption of oxygen. [BL-2] +
PankusDesk answer

Wet corrosion is electrochemical attack in a conducting aqueous or moist environment. Different metals, impurities or unequal oxygen access create anodic and cathodic regions. Metal dissolution occurs at the anode; reduction occurs at the cathode.

For iron the anodic reaction is \(\mathrm{Fe}\rightarrow\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}+2\mathrm e^-\). Electrons pass through the metal to cathodic regions; ionic conduction through the electrolyte completes the cell.

In an acidic environment, the cathodic reaction is hydrogen evolution: \(2\mathrm H^++2\mathrm e^-\rightarrow\mathrm H_2\uparrow\). Adding the half-reactions gives \(\mathrm{Fe}+2\mathrm H^+\rightarrow\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}+\mathrm H_2\uparrow\).

In neutral/slightly alkaline aerated water, oxygen reduction occurs: \(\dfrac12\mathrm O_2+\mathrm H_2\mathrm O+2\mathrm e^-\rightarrow2\mathrm{OH}^-\). Ferrous ions then precipitate: \(\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}+2\mathrm{OH}^-\rightarrow\mathrm{Fe(OH)}_2\).

Further oxidation gives \(4\mathrm{Fe(OH)}_2+\mathrm O_2+2\mathrm H_2\mathrm O\rightarrow4\mathrm{Fe(OH)}_3\). Rust is hydrated ferric oxide/oxyhydroxide material; the supplied notes also write hydrated Fe₂O₃. It does not form a durable protective barrier.

Hydrogen evolution and oxygen absorption are alternative cathodic mechanisms depending on environment. The anodic region loses iron in both cases; the cathodic region supports reduction.

(b) Differential metal (galvanic) corrosion

When dissimilar metals are electrically connected in a conducting corrosive environment, they form a galvanic cell. The more active metal becomes the anode and dissolves; the less active metal supports cathodic reduction.

Essay 7 · Factors affecting corrosion Explain how the following factors affect the rate of corrosion. [BL-2]a) passivityb) position of the metalc) relative areas of anode and cathoded) pH +
PankusDesk answer
(a) Passivity

A thin adherent, non-porous surface film can make an active metal highly corrosion-resistant. The notes use aluminium and chromium-containing alloys as examples. The film must remain stable in the environment; passivity can be lost when the film is damaged or conditions change.

(b) Position of the metal

For dissimilar metals in electrical contact in an electrolyte, the more active metal is anodic. A greater separation in the relevant galvanic series generally increases the driving force for attack of that member. Actual behaviour also depends on film formation and environment.

(c) Relative anode and cathode areas

A small anode connected to a large cathode has a high anodic current density and rapid local metal loss. The supplied example is a small steel pipe connected to a large copper tank. Avoid this area combination when designing galvanic couples.

(d) pH

The supplied notes state that acidic media are generally more corrosive to metals readily attacked by acid. Increasing pH can reduce their attack, but behaviour depends on the metal. The given example is zinc: rapid corrosion below pH 5 and minimum corrosion around pH 11 in the stated source.

Essay 8 · Differential aeration: waterline and pitting What is differential aeration corrosion? Explain the following with an example. [BL-2]a) water line corrosionb) pitting corrosion. +
PankusDesk answer

Differential-aeration corrosion arises from unequal oxygen concentration at different parts of the same metal surface. Less-aerated regions become anodic and dissolve; more-aerated regions become cathodic and support oxygen reduction.

(a) Waterline corrosion

In a partially filled steel tank, oxygen reaches the surface/meniscus region more easily than the submerged region just beneath it. The less-aerated submerged area becomes anodic. Attack is concentrated just below the waterline; the more-aerated region supports cathodic reduction. The supplied examples include storage tanks and ships.

(b) Pitting corrosion

Under a dust particle, deposit or damaged protective film, oxygen access is restricted. The covered small region becomes an anode while the larger exposed surface is cathodic. Metal dissolves locally and a pit develops.

The notes give machinery parts, oil-tank bottoms and chloride-exposed stainless steel, titanium or aluminium alloys as examples. Remove deposits, keep surfaces clean/smooth, and design structures to reduce crevices and deposit accumulation. The original waterline and pitting figures are included below.

Essay 9 · Cathodic protection methods Explain cathodic protection method for controlling corrosion./ Define cathodic protection. Explain sacrificial anodic protection and impressed current cathodic protection. +
PankusDesk answer

Cathodic protection suppresses corrosion of the structure by making it the cathode of an electrochemical system. The two supplied methods are sacrificial-anode protection and impressed-current protection.

1 Sacrificial-anode method

Connect the steel structure to a more active metal such as Mg, Zn or Al in the same electrolyte. The added metal oxidizes and supplies electrons; the steel becomes cathodic and is protected. Replace the anode when consumed.

Examples: zinc blocks on steel ship hulls and anodes for buried pipelines, cables, tanks and marine structures. Advantages include simple operation without an external DC source and relatively low installation/operating cost. Limitations include finite driving potential, anode consumption and replacement/monitoring.

The source composite below preserves the three sacrificial-protection arrangements.

2 Impressed-current method

Use an external DC supply or rectifier. Connect its negative terminal to the structure and positive terminal to the auxiliary anode in the electrolyte. The applied current makes the structure cathodic, opposing its anodic dissolution.

The notes list graphite, high-silicon iron and other suitable auxiliary anodes. Backfill may improve electrical contact with soil. Applications include larger water/oil pipelines, tanks and other extensive steel structures.

The output can be controlled and larger structures protected, but an external power supply, wiring, monitoring and maintenance are necessary. Initial cost and vulnerability of anode leads are limitations. The original pipeline/rectifier diagram is included below; its faint lines are a source limitation.

Bare structures can receive cathodic protection. Limited driving voltage, current demand, electrolyte resistance and anode consumption constrain sacrificial systems; the source limitations have been corrected with originals retained in the ledger.

Unnumbered source prompt · dry corrosion a) Discuss about chemical (Dry) Corrosion involving: Oxidation corrosion, Corrosion by other gases, Liquid metal corrosion +
PankusDesk answer
1 Oxidation corrosion

Oxygen directly attacks the metal to form an oxide. The resulting film controls further attack: a stable adherent non-porous film can protect, whereas a porous or volatile film permits continued attack. The notes list stable, unstable, volatile and porous oxide layers, and introduce the Pilling–Bedworth volume ratio.

Retain the source’s oxide examples and W/Cr/Ni illustration as attributed source material. The general formula and high-volume-ratio conclusion have qualifications recorded in the notes.

2 Corrosion by other gases

Gases such as SO₂, Cl₂, H₂S, F₂ and CO₂ may attack metals directly. The chemical affinity of the gas for the metal and the film’s nature determine the extent of attack. A protective non-porous product can limit reaction; a porous or volatile product permits deeper attack. The supplied examples are AgCl formed by chlorine on silver and SnCl₄ formed by chlorine on tin.

3 Liquid-metal corrosion

At high temperatures, flowing liquid metal can attack a solid metal/alloy. The supplied notes identify dissolution of the solid in the liquid metal and penetration of liquid metal into the solid as mechanisms. Both weaken the solid; nuclear-power equipment is the supplied application context.
